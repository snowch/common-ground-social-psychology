c01s03 | Personal qualities meet circumstances.
Social psychology studies how people’s thoughts, feelings, and behavior are shaped by other people and social circumstances. To explain an action, consider both the person and the situation.

Personal qualities include dispositions, goals, beliefs, and skills. A disposition is a usual tendency over time, such as patience. Someone described as having a sweet disposition is generally kind and good-natured. A tendency need not appear in every situation.

A situation is the set of circumstances someone faces, including other people, immediate demands, and available choices. Personal qualities and circumstances can interact: the same person may respond differently as those demands change.

In Maya’s story, Leo’s judgment about her commitment leaves the obstacles she faced unexplored. The late arrival alone cannot settle how much either personal qualities or circumstances contributed.

c01s04 | Observation and attribution.
Leo saw Maya arrive after the meeting had started. That observation describes what happened. It does not, by itself, explain why.

An attribution is an explanation of what caused an action.

A dispositional attribution locates a cause in characteristics of the person, such as goals, attitudes, or enduring tendencies. Leo’s claim that Maya did not care is a dispositional explanation.

A situational attribution locates a cause in circumstances, such as demands, constraints, or available opportunities. The broken lift and helping a neighbor offer situational explanations of the delay.

Personal characteristics and circumstances can contribute together. Compare the evidence for each explanation before deciding how much weight it deserves.

c01s04b | When a constraint gets too little weight.
The fundamental attribution error describes a tendency to give too much weight to personal characteristics and too little to circumstances when explaining another person’s behavior. Its strength varies with culture and context.

Correspondence bias is a related, more specific idea: inferring an enduring disposition from behavior that can be explained by situational constraints. The terms overlap, but they do not identify every possible error in a causal explanation.

Leo treats Maya’s late arrival as a sign of low commitment before investigating what delayed her. The broken lift and helping a neighbor make that conclusion less secure. These circumstances do not establish that personal characteristics played no part.

The café story illustrates a possible inference to examine. It does not measure an attribution bias. A bias label alone cannot establish how Leo reached his judgment.

c01s05 | Actor–observer differences need qualification.
Attribution research examines how people explain the causes of actions and events. Those explanations do not, by themselves, settle the cause of one person’s action.

The actor–observer hypothesis proposes that people explain their own actions more through situations and other people’s actions more through personal characteristics. Leo citing a bus delay for his own lateness but Maya’s priorities for hers illustrates this hypothesis.

Actor–observer differences are not a general rule. A meta-analysis did not support a general asymmetry across positive and negative events; results varied with the event and research method. Keep the hypothesis, the research finding, and the fictional example distinct.

c02s04b | Significance and usefulness are different questions.
Statistical significance concerns how unusual a result would be under a specified statistical model, often one assuming no difference between groups. It does not measure whether a difference is useful.

A small attendance increase could be statistically significant in a large study, yet be too small to justify an expensive campaign. Conversely, an imprecise estimate may leave a useful effect uncertain.

A nonsignificant result does not prove zero effect. Inspect the estimated size, its uncertainty, the study design, and other tests before deciding what the café should do.

c02s05 | What can we really conclude?
Replication repeats a test to examine how dependable a finding is. Independent tests help us learn whether a result holds up elsewhere.

Preregistration records the planned methods and analysis before results are known. That advance record reduces some opportunities for selective reporting. Preregistration cannot rescue a poor design.

With a sound design, a larger sample can narrow the uncertainty around an estimate. More observations cannot fix a systematically unfair comparison. Sharing the study’s methods and materials lets others inspect how it was done. A memorable study is a starting point for evaluation, rather than a final verdict.
