CHAPTER 1: The person and the room
Learning outcome: Given an ambiguous action, generate two situational explanations and identify evidence that distinguishes them from a personal explanation.

c01s01 | Story | Maya arrives late. The room has already decided why.
A neighborhood team is turning an empty shop into a repair café. At the first meeting, Maya arrives twenty minutes late. Leo takes her lateness as a sign that she doesn’t care about the project.

What Leo hasn’t seen is the broken lift at Maya’s apartment, or the neighbor she helped down the stairs.

Leo’s conclusion—that Maya didn’t care—was one possible explanation for her lateness. But he hadn’t considered what might have delayed her.

First, examine Leo’s reasoning in this fictional story. What did Leo observe about Maya’s arrival, and what did he infer about her priorities?

Then think of a recent occasion when you explained someone’s behavior. What did you observe directly, and which cause did you infer?

Throughout this course, keep that distinction in mind.

c01s02 | Your decision | What would you do?
Before the explanation, make your own choice.

Leo is assigning the opening shift. What is his most informative next move?

Consider the consequences of each option.
  1. Ask Maya about today and check her availability.
  2. Use today’s lateness as the main sign of reliability.
  3. Ignore today and assume she will arrive on time.
  Suggested answer: 1
  Feedback: Asking Maya about the delay lets Leo investigate the obstacle and plan the shift without ignoring the lateness.
  Feedback: One episode of lateness is a weak guide to how reliably someone usually arrives.
  Feedback: Assuming Maya will arrive on time does not investigate today’s delay or check her future availability.

c01s03 | Person and situation | Personal qualities meet circumstances.
Social psychology studies how people’s thoughts, feelings, and behavior are shaped by other people and social circumstances. To explain an action, consider both the person and the situation.

Personal qualities include dispositions, goals, beliefs, and skills. A disposition is a usual tendency over time, such as patience. Someone described as having a sweet disposition is generally kind and good-natured. A tendency need not appear in every situation.

A situation is the set of circumstances someone faces, including other people, immediate demands, and available choices. Personal qualities and circumstances can interact: the same person may respond differently as those demands change.

In Maya’s story, Leo’s judgment about her commitment leaves the obstacles she faced unexplored. The late arrival alone cannot settle how much either personal qualities or circumstances contributed.

c01s03b | Worked example | The same person, under pressure.
Imagine a volunteer who is usually patient with visitors. During a quiet shift, the volunteer listens and explains each repair calmly.

At the next shift, several visitors ask for help at once. The same volunteer gives one sharp reply. Their usual patience and the immediate pressure are both relevant to understanding that action.

Before judging the volunteer’s character, what changed in the room?

The immediate demands increased. That change offers a situational explanation to investigate. It does not establish that personal qualities played no part.

This example illustrates an interaction between a disposition and a situation. Compare the volunteer’s responses across calmer and busier shifts before drawing a conclusion about a lasting personal tendency.

c01s04 | Observation and attribution | Observation is not explanation.
Leo saw Maya arrive after the meeting had started. That observation describes what happened. It does not, by itself, explain why.

An attribution is an explanation of what caused an action.

Leo’s claim that Maya did not care about the project is an attribution. He has proposed a cause, rather than directly observed her priorities.

Maya can provide information about the delay. The broken lift and helping a neighbor offer possible causes that Leo had not considered. He needs evidence to compare those accounts.

For an action in your own life, first describe what you observed. Then state your proposed cause separately. Keeping those two statements distinct makes the explanation easier to test.

c01s04b | Attribution error | When the person gets too much weight.
Now consider how much weight Leo gives each kind of explanation. He treats Maya’s late arrival mainly as evidence about her personal qualities, before examining the circumstances that delayed her.

The fundamental attribution error is the tendency to give too much weight to personal qualities and too little to the situation when explaining another person’s behavior. Its strength varies with culture and context.

In Maya’s case, the broken lift and helping a neighbor make Leo’s initial explanation less complete. Personal qualities can still matter, but he needs to consider the circumstances before deciding how well his account fits.

To check an explanation you form, ask which situational causes you have investigated and which you may have overlooked. Naming a bias is a prompt to examine evidence; the label alone does not prove that someone’s reasoning is biased.

c01s05 | Evidence check | One observer, two explanations.
An attribution is an explanation of what caused an action.

Attribution research describes patterns across judgments. Those patterns do not settle the cause of one person’s action.

Actor–observer differences compare how one observer explains their own actions and other people’s actions. In a hypothetical comparison, Leo might cite a bus delay for his own lateness but Maya’s priorities for hers. The pattern varies with the judgment and study design.

c01s05b | Check an explanation | Test the explanation.
Return to Maya’s late arrival at the café. Leo has proposed that she did not care about the project. How could he check that explanation?

Begin with what was observed: Maya arrived after the start. Leo’s claim that she did not care adds a cause. Ask Maya about the delay before deciding which explanation fits.

Compare repeated occasions and settings. Is Maya late across different shifts, or only when a particular obstacle is present? A personal tendency becomes more plausible when the behavior recurs across varied circumstances. One episode is a weak basis for that conclusion.

Check alternatives. A broken lift or helping a neighbor could explain this delay. Identify what evidence would strengthen or weaken each account, then revise your confidence.

If you are reviewing someone else’s reasoning, ask what that person observed and considered. Disagreement alone does not establish bias. When you form your own interpretation, use these same steps to test it.

Choose an action and explain why it addresses the problem.
  1. Whether personality alone predicts attendance.
  2. Whether another travel plan improves attendance.
  3. Whether the missed shifts can be deleted.
  Suggested answer: 2
  Feedback: Focusing on personality alone leaves the link between bus cancellations and missed shifts untested.
  Feedback: Trying another travel plan tests the suspected barrier while keeping the attendance expectation clear.
  Feedback: Deleting the missed shifts from the record would hide the pattern. It would not remove the travel barrier.

CHAPTER 2: The poster that “worked”
Learning outcome: Identify a confound in a before-and-after claim and propose a fairer comparison.

c02s01 | Story | A brighter poster. More visitors. A cause?
Leo replaces the café’s plain poster with a colorful one. Attendance rises the next weekend. Leo credits the new poster for the increase.

But other things changed too. The rain stopped, the local paper ran a story, and the opening hours changed.

The team knows that more people attended. The team does not yet know how much the poster contributed. Keep that distinction in mind whenever someone says that one change caused another.

c02s02 | Your decision | What would you do?
Before the explanation, make your own choice.

Which statement fits Leo’s evidence?

Consider the consequences of each option.
  1. The poster caused the increase.
  2. The poster had no effect.
  3. Attendance rose; several causes remain possible.
  Suggested answer: 3
  Feedback: Crediting the poster alone overlooks the weather, publicity, and opening hours that changed at the same time.
  Feedback: Uncertainty about the poster’s contribution does not establish that the poster had no effect.
  Feedback: Saying that attendance rose preserves the observation. Leaving the cause open reflects the limits of the comparison.

c02s03 | Correlation and causation | Together does not establish because.
Correlation means that two things vary together. In the café story, the poster changed and attendance increased. That pattern is an observation to explain.

Leo’s claim that the poster caused the increase goes further. Better weather, publicity, or changed opening hours could also have brought visitors. The before-and-after pattern does not separate those causes.

When you assess a causal claim, distinguish the observed change from the proposed explanation. Then ask what comparison could help test that explanation.

c02s03b | Worked example | Several changes, one result.
A confound is another factor that could explain an apparent relationship. Leo changed the poster, but the weather improved and the local paper also covered the café.

Because these changes occurred together, the team cannot tell how much of the attendance rise came from the poster. A detailed story about the new design does not remove that problem.

A fairer comparison would vary the invitation while keeping the event and practical details as similar as possible. The next lesson distinguishes selecting participants from assigning the versions they receive.

c02s03c | Design a fair test | Select people. Assign conditions.
Random sampling and random assignment answer different questions. Sampling concerns who enters the study. Assignment concerns which condition each selected person receives.

The café could randomly select households from a neighborhood list, then use another chance process to assign the first invitation design or the second for the same event. Random assignment helps balance other influences between groups on average; it does not guarantee identical groups.

Testing only existing supporters may tell the team little about residents who have never visited. Randomly assigning those supporters to versions would not solve that sampling limitation.

c02s04 | Size and uncertainty | How large is the difference?
Imagine two groups of one hundred households. Twelve attend after receiving invitation A, and fifteen attend after receiving invitation B. These are invented teaching numbers.

Attendance differs by three percentage points: fifteen percent minus twelve percent. A different sample could produce a different result, so this estimate has uncertainty.

The café needs to consider the size and precision of the estimated difference alongside the cost of changing its invitation. Whether a statistical test calls the result significant is a separate question.

c02s04b | Interpret a result | Significance and usefulness are different questions.
Statistical significance concerns how unusual a result would be under a specified statistical model, often one assuming no difference between groups. It does not measure whether a difference is useful.

A small attendance increase could be statistically significant in a large study, yet be too small to justify an expensive campaign. Conversely, an imprecise estimate may leave a useful effect uncertain.

A nonsignificant result does not prove zero effect. Inspect the estimated size, its uncertainty, the study design, and other tests before deciding what the café should do.

c02s05 | Evidence check | What can we really conclude?
Replication repeats a test to examine how dependable a finding is. Independent tests help us learn whether a result holds up elsewhere.

Preregistration records the planned methods and analysis before results are known. That advance record reduces some opportunities for selective reporting. Preregistration cannot rescue a poor design.

With a sound design, a larger sample can narrow the uncertainty around an estimate. More observations cannot fix a systematically unfair comparison. Sharing the study’s methods and materials lets others inspect how it was done. A memorable study is a starting point for evaluation, rather than a final verdict.

Choose an action and explain why it addresses the problem.

  2. Randomize designs for one event; define attendance in advance.
  3. Design A for existing fans; design B for newcomers.
  Suggested answer: 2
  Feedback: Comparing winter with summer changes the season alongside the invitation design.
  Feedback: Randomizing invitations for one event improves the comparison. Also check whether households share invitations.
  Feedback: Existing fans and newcomers differ in prior interest. That difference could affect attendance regardless of the invitation.

CHAPTER 3: The story that comes to mind
Learning outcome: Identify a missing base rate and formulate a question that could challenge an initial belief.

c03s01 | Story | One broken toaster becomes “they always break.”
A repaired toaster fails again. The story spreads through the café chat. Leo concludes that electrical repairs usually fail, although nobody has counted the outcomes.

Maya remembers grateful visitors and overlooks returns. Maya therefore has a more positive impression than Leo.

Both people remember genuine cases. Neither person has systematically checked all comparable repairs. To estimate how often repairs fail, the team needs a record that includes the cases nobody talked about.

c03s02 | Your decision | What would you do?
Before the explanation, make your own choice.

What most improves the estimate of repeat failures?

Consider the consequences of each option.
  1. A detailed account of the worst failure.
  2. Failures divided by comparable completed repairs.
  3. A vote on whose impression feels realistic.
  Suggested answer: 2
  Feedback: A failed repair can help explain how a fault occurs. One case cannot establish how often repairs fail.
  Feedback: The total of comparable repairs provides the denominator for a failure rate. Consistent follow-up still matters.
  Feedback: A vote measures the team’s beliefs. The voters may share the same gaps in information.

c03s03 | First lens | Memorable is not the same as common.
Heuristics are mental shortcuts. They can help when time and information are limited. The availability shortcut uses examples that come easily to mind. After hearing the toaster story, Leo may judge repair failures by how easily he remembers one.

Vivid or recent examples can be a poor guide to frequency. A complaint channel helps discover faults, but usually says little about repairs that worked.

To estimate a failure rate, count failures and the total of comparable repairs. In an invented example, four failures among forty repairs means ten percent. Forty is the denominator: the total used to interpret the four failures. Without that total and consistent follow-up, the café cannot tell how common failure is.

c03s04 | Confirmation bias | Give a belief a fair test.
Confirmation bias means seeking or interpreting information in ways that fit an existing belief. If Leo thinks evening sessions fail, asking only about unsuccessful evenings gives that belief an unfair advantage.

A fairer check defines the outcome first and records comparable successes and failures. Someone with a different expectation can review whether the same standard was applied to every case.

For your own reasoning, ask what evidence could change your initial view. The aim is to make the belief testable. Naming a bias alone does not establish that a conclusion is wrong.

c03s04b | Motivated reasoning | When a desired answer feels persuasive.
Motivated reasoning concerns how a conclusion we want to reach influences our reasoning. Maya wants the café to succeed, so reassuring repair stories may feel easier to accept than reports of returns.

The desired conclusion could still be correct. To investigate it, Maya can define a repair failure in advance, follow up comparable repairs consistently, and invite someone to challenge the initial interpretation.

With confirmation bias, the question is whether evidence was selected to fit an existing belief. With motivated reasoning, the question is whether a preferred conclusion shaped that evaluation. The processes can overlap. Test the evidence; a label alone does not settle the issue.

c03s05 | Evidence check | What can we really conclude?
When assessing someone else’s estimate, compare that estimate with a relevant record before proposing that it is biased. A bias label alone cannot establish how the person reached the estimate.

For example, Leo’s vivid repair stories might give a distorted impression of failure. In another case, an experienced repairer’s remembered cases might accurately reflect a dependable record. Representative evidence helps distinguish these possibilities.

When reviewing another person’s reasoning, ask which information they used and which cases they missed. Disagreeing with their estimate does not replace that investigation. Experts also need feedback and opportunities to revise their judgments.

  Suggested answer: 3

  Feedback: Agreement with Leo may reflect shared assumptions rather than independent evidence.
  Feedback: Naming outcomes that would count as success gives Leo’s belief a chance to be wrong.

CHAPTER 4: The plan we cannot let go
Learning outcome: Distinguish dissonance reduction from evidence-based revision and compare future options without counting irrecoverable costs as benefits.

c04s01 | Story | “I chose it” quietly becomes “it must be right.”
Maya championed a booking system. After weeks of work, the system still loses reservations. Maya usually spots technical problems quickly, but now explains each failure away.

For Maya, admitting that the system is poor feels like admitting that she is poor at her role. Leo proposes another month of configuration because the team has already spent so much.

Maya is defending her judgment. Leo is focused on money already spent. Both need to compare what the available options would deliver from today onward.

c04s02 | Your decision | What would you do?
Before the explanation, make your own choice.

What makes honest revision easier?

Consider the consequences of each option.
  1. Review failures and compare options afresh.
  2. Remind Maya that a competent coordinator would know better.
  3. Keep the system until the investment feels worthwhile.
  Suggested answer: 1
  Feedback: Reviewing failures and comparing options turns attention toward outcomes and the next decision.
  Feedback: Questioning Maya’s competence can increase defensiveness without improving the comparison of options.
  Feedback: Continuing with an inferior option cannot recover money that has already been spent.

c04s03 | First lens | Inconsistency can feel uncomfortable.
Cognitive dissonance theory concerns a clash between things we believe or know about our actions. “Cognitions” is the technical term for those beliefs and pieces of knowledge. The theory proposes that such a clash can create discomfort we are motivated to reduce.

Maya believes she chooses reliable tools, yet the booking system she chose keeps losing reservations. She could reduce that discomfort by dismissing each failure, or by revising her view: a capable coordinator can recognize and correct a poor choice.

People may change a behavior, change a belief, or add a justification. The response is not predetermined. How much choice and responsibility someone feels can affect the pressure to justify an action.

c04s04 | Second lens | Give the future a fresh vote.
A sunk cost is money, time, or effort already spent that cannot be recovered. The sunk cost effect occurs when that past expense influences a decision about what to do next.

The hours spent configuring the booking system cannot be recovered by continuing to use it. The next decision concerns future bookings, support costs, and the effort needed to switch. Compare the future benefits and avoidable costs of each option.

Past work can still matter through its future consequences. Skills the team can reuse are a benefit; retraining after a switch is a future cost. Those consequences belong in the comparison. The wish to make past effort feel worthwhile does not, by itself, favor continuing.

c04s05 | Evidence check | What can we really conclude?
Evidence for specific cognitive dissonance demonstrations is uneven. In 2024, researchers repeated one procedure across multiple laboratories. Participants were asked to argue for a position they did not hold, with more or less choice about doing so. This is called an induced-compliance procedure.

The study tested whether the amount of choice affected later attitudes. Its primary analysis did not support the predicted difference between the higher-choice and lower-choice groups.

That result challenges the particular procedure tested. It does not settle every version of cognitive dissonance theory. Distinguish the broad explanation of discomfort and justification from the evidence for a particular prediction. The linked Vaidis and colleagues study provides the details.

CHAPTER 5: The quiet vote
Learning outcome: Distinguish normative and informational influence and design a process that makes independent concerns visible.

c05s01 | Story | Everyone says yes. Has everyone agreed?
A donor offers a publicity event. The chair calls it a wonderful opportunity, and the first three volunteers agree. Sam doubts that the team can manage the workload, but stays silent.

Sam assumes Maya has checked the risks. Maya reads Sam’s silence as reassurance. Each person is taking the other’s outward response as a sign that the proposal has been considered carefully.

Public agreement can hide private doubts. In a later decision in this chapter, the chair asks volunteers to skip a required equipment check. You will consider how the team can question that instruction before unsafe work begins.

c05s02 | Your decision | What would you do?
Before the explanation, make your own choice.

What could reveal concerns before discussion?

Consider the consequences of each option.
  1. Have the chair announce the preferred answer.
  2. Collect independent written judgments first.
  3. Ask only experienced members to speak.
  Suggested answer: 2
  Feedback: Announcing the chair’s preferred answer first can pull later comments toward it, making independent concerns harder to see.
  Feedback: Independent written judgments let people record a view before seeing which answer has public support.
  Feedback: Experience matters, but other members may have relevant information.

c05s03 | First lens | Two reasons to follow a group.
Informational influence means treating other people’s responses as evidence about reality. Sam might think, “Maya agreed, so perhaps she knows we can manage the event.” This is especially relevant when Sam is uncertain.

Normative influence concerns acceptance and the wish to avoid rejection. Sam might think, “I’m worried about the workload, but I don’t want the team to see me as unhelpful.”

Both influences can operate at once. Sam may doubt their own estimate and fear disappointing the chair.

For your own group decisions, check the information behind the agreement and whether members can disagree without a social penalty. Those checks help you evaluate the decision without assuming that you know everyone’s private motive.

c05s04 | Compliance and obedience | A request and an authority’s order.
Compliance means responding to a request. If Leo asks Sam to cover a shift and Sam agrees, Sam has complied. Obedience means responding to an authority’s order. Following the chair’s instruction because the chair is in charge illustrates obedience.

A request from an authority can involve both processes. The role may make the demand feel legitimate or encourage someone to think that the chair is responsible for the decision.

That pressure does not make obedience inevitable. People can question or resist. Designing a usable way to raise a concern is the practical task in the next lesson.

c05s04b | Question an instruction | Make questioning possible before work continues.
In the café decision, the chair asks volunteers to bypass a required equipment check. The team needs a practical route for questioning that instruction while the decision still matters.

An agreed rule can allow a volunteer to pause unsafe work, name the concern, and bring it to the person responsible under the team’s procedures. The response must reflect the relevant training and responsibilities.

An invitation to speak up is less useful if nobody knows what happens next. Evaluate whether people can actually raise concerns before work continues. An authority’s instruction does not remove accountability.

c05s05 | Evidence check | What can we really conclude?
In Asch’s line-judgment studies, participants compared line lengths after hearing a group give incorrect answers. The studies examined when people would follow a majority despite the visible evidence.

In Milgram’s studies, an experimenter urged participants to continue a task they believed delivered electric shocks to another person. The studies examined responses to apparent authority in a highly structured setting.

Both sets of studies raise questions about methods, ethics, and how widely their findings apply. Their results concern particular conditions of influence; they are not universal obedience rates. Some participants resisted, and responses varied with the conditions. Harmful conduct is not inevitable.

CHAPTER 6: The invitation
Learning outcome: Evaluate a message by its evidence, audience needs, and honest description of social norms.

c06s01 | Story | Two invitations. Different reasons to care.
The café needs volunteers. Leo drafts a glossy invitation featuring a local celebrity. Maya’s invitation explains what volunteers can learn, how much time a shift takes, and what support is available.

A hurried passerby might notice the celebrity. A person deciding whether to volunteer regularly needs practical details about the commitment.

An invitation can attract attention and help someone evaluate a decision. The team needs to consider both jobs, while keeping the invitation honest.

c06s02 | Your decision | What would you do?
Before the explanation, make your own choice.

A potential volunteer worries about the time commitment. What information would help most?

Consider the consequences of each option.
  1. A second celebrity endorsement.
  2. Shift length, support, and an optional trial.
  3. A stronger claim that this is unmissable.
  Suggested answer: 2
  Feedback: A celebrity endorsement may attract attention while leaving the volunteer’s time concern unanswered.
  Feedback: Shift length, support, and an optional trial give the volunteer relevant information about the commitment.
  Feedback: Calling the opportunity unmissable adds pressure without answering how much time it requires.

c06s03 | Attitude and action | An evaluation is not a sign-up.
An attitude is an evaluation, such as judging volunteering to be worthwhile or burdensome. An invitation may influence that evaluation, but a positive attitude does not guarantee a sign-up.

A resident might value the café yet be unable to attend its shifts. Time, transport, available support, and personal choice also affect what the person does.

When evaluating Maya’s invitation, distinguish a favorable reaction from understanding the commitment and choosing to participate. Ask what outcome the message is intended to support before deciding whether it worked.

c06s03b | Message processing | How carefully is the message examined?
The elaboration likelihood model describes how motivation and ability affect examination of a persuasive message. Central processing involves considering relevant arguments, such as shift length, available support, and the likely contribution.

Peripheral processing gives more weight to cues such as a familiar face or an apparently credible speaker. A hurried passerby might respond favorably to the celebrity without checking practical details.

These are differences in how a message is processed, not fixed personality types or two sealed categories. The same person may examine different messages more or less carefully. Argument quality matters more when someone can and wants to think carefully.

c06s04 | Descriptive and injunctive norms | Check the truth of a norm claim.
A descriptive norm concerns what people do. Saying that many residents volunteer makes a descriptive-norm claim. An injunctive norm concerns approval: saying the community values volunteering makes an injunctive-norm claim.

Both claims need evidence. Inventing a norm is misleading. Highlighting how common an unwanted behavior is can sometimes make that behavior seem normal.

Before putting a norm in the café’s invitation, check whether the team can substantiate it. A truthful description of the actual commitment can be more useful than unsupported social proof.

c06s04b | Write an honest invitation | Help someone make an informed choice.
Writing an honest invitation is a practical task. For the café, explain what a volunteer would contribute, how long a shift takes, and what support is available.

Make the next step clear. A potential volunteer could ask a question or try a supported shift before making a larger commitment. Leave room to decline without pressure.

Remove social-proof claims the team cannot substantiate. To evaluate the revised invitation, check understanding and informed participation, rather than relying only on attention or clicks.

c06s05 | Evidence check | What can we really conclude?
Persuasion depends on the audience, topic, source, and outcome being measured. A changed survey answer is not the same outcome as sustained behavior change.

For the café team, the next task is to evaluate its revised invitation. More clicks show increased attention; they do not establish that potential volunteers understood the commitment or will keep volunteering.

The team can track comprehension, informed sign-ups, and continued participation. To learn about the volunteers’ experience, ask them whether they understood the effort involved and felt able to decline. Their answers provide information that the organizers’ impressions alone cannot supply.

CHAPTER 7: When “we” takes over
Learning outcome: Identify a plausible group process behind a failure and select a procedure that addresses it.

c07s01 | Story | Two teams stop sharing a table.
Repair volunteers call the welcome desk “the talkers.” The welcome team calls the repair bench “the gatekeepers.” Each team notices its own effort more clearly than the other team’s contribution.

At a planning meeting, speakers propose increasingly ambitious events. Meanwhile, nobody owns the checklist. Each volunteer assumes someone else is responsible.

The café now faces several group problems: boundaries between teams, an escalating decision, and unclear task ownership. Each problem may need a different remedy.

Before the explanation, make your own choice.

Consider the consequences of each option.
  1. Agree on owners, deadlines, and needed support.
  2. Tell everyone their commitment is disappointing.
  3. Copy more people onto every task.
  Suggested answer: 1
  Feedback: Agreed owners, deadlines, and support make responsibility clearer and help people coordinate the work.
  Feedback: Telling everyone to be more committed still leaves the team unsure who owns each checklist task.
  Feedback: Copying more people onto a task can leave each volunteer assuming another recipient will handle it.

c07s03 | Social identity | Who counts as our team?
Group memberships help people understand who they are and who belongs with them. An in-group is a group someone sees as us. Giving repair volunteers first choice of shifts simply because they are our team illustrates in-group favoritism.

The welcome team’s contribution may then receive less recognition or influence. Favoritism can create unequal treatment without explicit hatred of outsiders.

When reviewing the café’s division, examine how membership affects actual decisions. A group label suggests a process to investigate; it does not reveal every member’s private motives.

c07s03b | Coordinate across teams | Make the shared goal workable.
A successful visitor experience needs both the welcome desk and the repair bench. A shared goal can help the teams see why both contributions matter.

Translate that goal into arrangements: who greets the visitor, who assesses the repair, what information moves between teams, and which tools and decisions each team can access. Review workloads and share credit.

A shared name alone does not resolve unequal power or conflicting incentives. If one team still controls the shift schedule and resources, renaming everyone the café team leaves those arrangements in place.

c07s04 | Group polarization | Discussion can strengthen an initial leaning.
Group polarization means moving toward a more extreme version of a group’s initial leaning. A team initially favoring a small event might finish discussion demanding a week-long festival. Supporting arguments and comparisons with enthusiastic members can contribute.

Simply agreeing on a similar small event would be consensus. Agreement and polarization can also occur together; the key question is how the positions changed.

To examine the café’s discussion, compare members’ independent views before and after it. An ambitious final budget alone cannot establish which process produced it.

c07s04b | Effort and coordination | Poor output can have different causes.
Social loafing means reducing individual effort when working collectively. A volunteer might check fewer items because their contribution will be hidden in the team’s total.

Coordination loss is different. Two willing volunteers may duplicate one repair while another waits for a tool. Genuine effort is being poorly combined.

Before choosing a remedy for the unfinished checklist, investigate effort, resources, sequencing, and task ownership. Clear roles or better handovers may help a coordination problem; accusing volunteers of low commitment would not establish its cause.

c07s05 | Evidence check | What can we really conclude?
Groupthink describes cases where pressure for agreement interferes with critical discussion. A poor decision alone does not establish that this process occurred.

To review the café’s planning meeting, look at what happened during the discussion. Which concerns were withheld? Which information was repeated or missed? Were roles or incentives unclear?

Then choose a change that addresses the process you found. Independent estimates can broaden the evidence. Clear roles can improve coordination. Structured disagreement can make concerns easier to express. These changes help the team investigate the decision without pretending to know every member’s private thoughts.

CHAPTER 8: The invisible doorway
Learning outcome: Distinguish stereotypes, prejudice, and discrimination and locate a procedural barrier to access.

c08s01 | Story | “Anyone can join.” The form says otherwise.
The café says everyone is welcome. Yet recruitment runs through existing friends, meetings happen during working hours, and the application assumes that people own specialist tools.

A newcomer is treated as inexperienced before anyone checks the applicant’s skills. The barrier may involve a judgment about the person, a recruitment rule, or both.

To understand unequal access, examine beliefs, attitudes, individual decisions, and procedures. Good intentions alone do not tell us how the process works.

c08s02 | Your decision | What would you do?

  2. Trace who can enter each stage and where people drop out.
  3. Add a fairness slogan to the poster.
  Suggested answer: 2
  Feedback: Organizers’ beliefs about their own fairness do not establish who can use the recruitment process.
  Feedback: Tracing each stage can reveal access barriers that intentions and slogans miss.
  Feedback: The slogan does not change the entry requirements.

c08s03 | Stereotype, prejudice, discrimination | Separate belief, attitude, and action.
A stereotype is a generalized belief about a group, such as assuming that every newcomer lacks repair skills. Prejudice is an attitude toward a group, often involving dislike: resenting newcomers before meeting them.

Discrimination is unequal treatment based on group membership, such as rejecting an applicant because they are a newcomer without checking relevant skills.

Beliefs, attitudes, and actions can overlap, but evidence about one does not establish everything about the others. A decision is evidence to examine; it does not reveal an organizer’s entire belief system. The recruitment process also needs inspection.

c08s03b | Inspect access barriers | Follow the route into participation.
The café says everyone is welcome, yet recruitment runs through existing friends, meetings happen during working hours, and applicants are expected to own specialist tools. Each arrangement can restrict who enters.

Trace the route from hearing about the opportunity to applying and participating. Ask where people are excluded or drop out, and investigate the reasons.

A barrier can remain without an openly hostile statement. Good intentions alone do not establish fair access. The observed process does not, by itself, diagnose an organizer’s attitude or character.

c08s04 | Intergroup contact | Contact needs meaningful participation.
Intergroup contact means interaction between members of different groups. It can reduce prejudice on average, though bringing people together does not guarantee improvement. Cooperation, common goals, equal status within the encounter, and institutional support matter.

Experienced volunteers and newcomers could work together on a repair, share decisions, and receive support from the coordinator. If newcomers only watch while established members control the tools, participation is less equal.

Evaluate the encounter people actually experience. Contact and changes to recruitment rules are different interventions, so each needs its own assessment.

c08s04b | Procedural change | Change the rule that creates the barrier.
A friendly contact event does not change a rule requiring applicants to own expensive tools. Removing that requirement where unnecessary is a procedural change.

The café could open applications beyond existing friends, use relevant skills criteria, and provide shared tools where feasible. Specify the barrier each change is intended to address.

Follow up who can apply, progress, and remain involved. Include applicants’ experiences, interpret small samples cautiously, and protect privacy. A revised form or a feeling of inclusiveness alone cannot establish that access improved.

c08s05 | Evidence check | What can we really conclude?
An explicit attitude is an evaluation someone reports directly. An implicit association is a link between ideas that may be measured indirectly, such as through the speed of responses in a sorting task. Discriminatory behavior concerns how people are actually treated.

When interpreting a participant’s implicit-association score, remember that it is not a complete diagnosis of that person’s character or a forecast of every action.

When evaluating a recruitment change, inspect access and participation: who can apply, progress, and remain involved? Include follow-up and the experiences of applicants. An organizer saying “I am fair” cannot establish that those outcomes are fair.

Interpret small samples cautiously and protect privacy. Investigating unequal access does not justify collecting unnecessary personal information.

CHAPTER 9: A place beside someone
Learning outcome: Explain how contact opportunities and reciprocal responsiveness can support closeness without guaranteeing it.

c09s01 | Story | Friendship begins with the quiet shift.
Sam and Leo take the quiet Thursday session. At first, they discuss only spare parts. Over several weeks, they discover a shared interest in old radios.

Leo mentions a difficult week. Sam listens before offering a solution, and Leo feels heard.

c09s02 | Your decision | What would you do?

  1. Offer a shared activity and notice reciprocal interest.
  2. Share increasingly private details until closeness develops.
  3. Keep arranging contact after invitations are declined.
  Suggested answer: 1
  Feedback: Offering a shared activity creates an opportunity to connect. Notice whether the other person also chooses to participate and shows interest.
  Feedback: Disclosure without mutual readiness can feel intrusive.
  Feedback: A refusal is information about a boundary, not a barrier to overcome.

c09s03 | Proximity and similarity | Shared settings create opportunities.
Daily settings affect whom people have opportunities to meet. Sharing the Thursday shift brings Sam and Leo into contact. Their interest in old radios gives them something to discuss.

Proximity creates opportunities, and similarity can make conversation easier. Neither guarantees attraction, compatibility, or a healthy relationship.

Notice reciprocal interest and respect expressed boundaries. Their shared shift creates opportunities for friendship; it creates no obligation to become friends. The effect of repeated familiarity is a related question in the next lesson.

c09s03b | Mere exposure | Familiarity has conditions and limits.
The mere exposure effect refers to cases where repeated encounters increase liking. A face that initially feels unfamiliar may become more comfortable to see.

That pattern has limits. Repeated unwanted contact can intensify annoyance or threat. More contact is not automatically better, and a general research pattern does not determine what Sam or Leo should want.

Use shared activities as opportunities for willing interaction. If someone declines an invitation or asks for space, respect that preference rather than treating familiarity as a reason to persist.

c09s04 | Disclosure and responsiveness | Closeness depends on the response.
Disclosure means sharing something personal. Leo mentioning a difficult week is a disclosure. Responsiveness means responding in a way that helps someone feel understood and cared for.

Sam could listen, check what Leo means, and ask whether listening, practical help, or advice would be useful. Disclosure can support intimacy when it receives an understanding response.

Reciprocity means consideration can flow both ways over time. It does not require matching secrets on demand or treating favors as debts. Trust, willingness, fairness, and commitment also matter.

c09s04b | Attachment and boundaries | Respond to the preference someone states.
People differ in how secure or anxious they feel about closeness. Attachment labels describe patterns studied in relationships; they are not diagnoses.

When Sam declines an invitation and asks for time alone, Leo has a stated preference to respond to. One refusal does not establish Sam’s attachment pattern or justify ignoring that preference.

Leo can accept the refusal and leave future contact optional. To understand a relationship, ask about the people’s actual experiences and wishes rather than inferring a complete explanation from a label.

c09s05 | Evidence check | What can we really conclude?
Much relationship research examines associations or asks people to report their own experiences. Self-reports provide information about those experiences, but people may remember events differently or answer in ways they prefer others to see. Samples may also represent only particular cultures or relationship forms.

Suppose similar partners report greater satisfaction. That association does not, by itself, establish that making two people more similar would improve their relationship. Other factors might contribute to both similarity and satisfaction.

To apply that research to Sam and Leo, first ask about their actual experiences and preferences. Their conversations may reveal relevant details that a group average cannot settle. A research pattern cannot decide what either person should want.

Learning outcome: Identify barriers to helping and design a specific, safe response with clear responsibility.

c10s01 | Story | A crash. A full room. A moment of hesitation.

  2. People may be uncertain and assume someone else will act.

  Feedback: Group size does not determine concern on its own.

c10s03 | Bystander processes | Two reasons concerned people may hesitate.
The bystander effect describes conditions where others’ presence reduces an individual’s likelihood of helping. Diffusion of responsibility is one possible process: a concerned volunteer assumes someone else will act.

Pluralistic ignorance concerns interpreting reactions. Leo might read Sam’s outward calm as reassurance that no help is needed, even while Sam is privately uncertain. He has mistaken an appearance for a private assessment.

Diffusion concerns who should act; pluralistic ignorance concerns how the need is interpreted. Both could contribute after the shelf falls. In other situations, people provide essential support. The effect depends on the setting.

c10s03b | Plan appropriate help | Give concern a workable next step.
Explaining hesitation and planning an appropriate response are different tasks. For a low-risk shared job, clarify the need, agree who will act, and check that the person has the necessary skills and support.

For example, before a café session, the team can name who checks a shared setup task and how that person reports an unresolved problem. A clear handover makes responsibility visible.

In an emergency, follow local guidance and your training and contact appropriate help. A social psychology model does not replace practical instruction. Evaluate the plan by whether appropriate help becomes available.

c10s04 | Aggression and de-escalation | Anger leaves a choice about action.
Aggression is behavior intended to harm. Anger is a feeling. A volunteer can feel angry about the fallen shelf and still respond constructively.

Frustration, perceived threat, situational pressure, and learned expectations can contribute to aggression. Those influences do not make harm inevitable or remove responsibility.

De-escalation means reducing tension and the risk of harm. At the café, space to calm down, clear conduct rules, and a trained person to contact may help. Evaluate those arrangements rather than treating them as guarantees.

c10s04b | Cooperation | Turn a shared aim into coordinated work.
Cooperation needs more than a general wish to work together. A shared task needs a clear outcome, named contributions, usable resources, and a way to report problems.

For the replacement shelf, the team can agree who owns the check, what support that person needs, and how an unresolved concern will be raised. Naming responsibility makes the next step visible.

Review the outcome and the fairness of benefits and burdens. Use feedback to revise arrangements that are not working. A statement that everyone cares does not establish that the plan is effective.

c10s05 | Evidence check | What can we really conclude?
Laboratory studies may measure aggression through controlled tasks, such as choosing an unpleasant noise for another participant. Such measures are imperfect indicators of serious violence outside the laboratory.

Bystander findings also vary with danger, relationships, and available support. “Crowds don’t help” hides those differences and cannot explain every incident.

For a real emergency, follow local guidance and your training. A social psychology explanation does not replace first-aid instruction. Evaluate practical changes by whether appropriate, safe help becomes more available. A team saying it cares does not establish that its response plan works.
