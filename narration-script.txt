CHAPTER 1: The person and the room
Learning outcome: Given an ambiguous action, generate two situational explanations and identify evidence that distinguishes them from a personal explanation.

c01s01 | Story | Maya arrives late. The room has already decided why.
A neighborhood team is turning an empty shop into a repair café. At the first meeting, Maya arrives twenty minutes late. Leo takes her lateness as a sign that she doesn’t care about the project.

What Leo hasn’t seen is the broken lift at Maya’s apartment, or the neighbor she helped down the stairs.

Leo’s conclusion—that Maya didn’t care—was one possible explanation for her lateness. But he hadn’t considered what might have delayed her.

These characters are fictional, but the question is familiar: what did you observe, and what explanation did you supply?

c01s02 | Your decision | What would you do?
Before the explanation, make your own choice.

Leo is assigning the opening shift. What is his most informative next move?

Consider the consequences of each option.
  1. Ask Maya about today and check her availability.
  2. Use today’s lateness as the main sign of reliability.
  3. Ignore today and assume she will arrive on time.
  Suggested answer: 1
  Feedback: Asking Maya about the delay lets Leo investigate the obstacle and plan the shift without ignoring the lateness.
  Feedback: One episode of lateness is a weak guide to how reliably someone usually arrives.
  Feedback: Avoiding judgment is not the same as gathering evidence.

c01s03 | First lens | Behavior has more than one author.
A disposition is a personal tendency that persists over time. Someone described as having a “sweet disposition” is generally kind and good-natured. A tendency need not appear in every situation.

A situation is the set of circumstances someone faces, including other people, immediate demands, and available choices. A usually patient volunteer might speak sharply when several visitors demand help at once. Both the volunteer’s usual patience and the pressure matter.

Social psychology studies how people’s thoughts, feelings, and behavior are shaped by other people and social circumstances. That includes how personal qualities and circumstances interact. Personal qualities include dispositions, but also goals, beliefs, and skills.

For Maya, the broken lift made arriving on time harder. Investigating that barrier helps the team explain her lateness and plan future shifts, while keeping commitments clear.

c01s04 | Second lens | Observation is not explanation.
When explaining someone else’s behavior, we can give too much weight to personal qualities and too little to circumstances. Psychologists call this tendency the fundamental attribution error. Its strength varies with culture and context.

To check your own judgment, describe the behavior neutrally. Generate alternative explanations, then look for evidence that could make each explanation less plausible. Disagreeing with someone’s judgment does not, by itself, establish that the judgment is biased.

c01s05 | Evidence check | What can we really conclude?
An attribution is an explanation of what caused an action. Saying “Maya arrived late” records an observation. Saying “Maya arrived late because she doesn’t care” adds an attribution about her priorities.

Attribution research describes patterns in how people explain behavior. It does not establish that personal qualities never matter. Repeated behavior across varied settings is more informative than one episode.

Researchers also examine actor–observer differences: differences between how we explain our own actions and other people’s actions. We might explain our own lateness through a delayed bus, but someone else’s through poor planning. Whether that pattern appears depends on the judgment and study design. A bias label alone cannot reveal what is happening in someone’s mind.

c01s06 | New situation | Try the idea somewhere new.
Apply the idea to a new decision.

Choose an action and explain why it addresses the problem.
  1. Whether personality alone predicts attendance.
  2. Whether another travel plan improves attendance.
  3. Whether the missed shifts can be deleted.
  Suggested answer: 2
  Feedback: Focusing on personality alone leaves the link between bus cancellations and missed shifts untested.
  Feedback: Trying another travel plan tests the suspected barrier while keeping the attendance expectation clear.
  Feedback: Deleting the missed shifts from the record would hide the pattern. It would not remove the travel barrier.

CHAPTER 2: The poster that “worked”
Learning outcome: Identify a confound in a before-and-after claim and propose a fairer comparison.

c02s01 | Story | A brighter poster. More visitors. A cause?
Leo replaces the café’s plain poster with a colorful one. Attendance rises the next weekend. Leo credits the new poster for the increase.

But other things changed too. The rain stopped, the local paper ran a story, and the opening hours changed.

The team knows that more people attended. The team does not yet know how much the poster contributed. Keep that distinction in mind whenever someone says that one change caused another.

c02s02 | Your decision | What would you do?
Before the explanation, make your own choice.

Which statement fits Leo’s evidence?

Consider the consequences of each option.
  1. The poster caused the increase.
  2. The poster had no effect.
  3. Attendance rose; several causes remain possible.
  Suggested answer: 3
  Feedback: Crediting the poster alone overlooks the weather, publicity, and opening hours that changed at the same time.
  Feedback: Uncertainty about the poster’s contribution does not establish that the poster had no effect.
  Feedback: Saying that attendance rose preserves the observation. Leaving the cause open reflects the limits of the comparison.

c02s03 | First lens | Comparison makes a claim testable.
Correlation means that two things vary together. A confound is another factor that could explain an apparent relationship. Attendance rose after Leo changed the poster, but better weather could also have brought more visitors. The poster comparison cannot separate those explanations.

An experiment deliberately changes a condition and compares outcomes. For one café event, a chance process could assign households to receive invitation A or B. This random assignment helps balance other influences between groups on average; it cannot guarantee identical groups.

Random sampling concerns who enters the study. Selecting households by chance from a neighborhood list is different from assigning invitation designs to households already selected. Testing only existing supporters may tell us little about residents who have never visited.

c02s04 | Second lens | Ask how much—and how uncertain.
Imagine two groups of one hundred households. Twelve attend after receiving one invitation design. Fifteen attend after receiving the other. These are invented teaching numbers.

Attendance is twelve percent in one group and fifteen percent in the other: a difference of three percentage points. A different sample could produce a different result, so the estimated advantage has uncertainty.

Statistical significance concerns how unusual a result would be under a specified statistical model, often one assuming no difference between groups. It does not tell us whether a difference matters in practice. A small change could be statistically significant in a large study, yet be too small to justify an expensive campaign.

A nonsignificant result does not prove zero effect. Examine the size of the estimate, its uncertainty, the study design, and other tests.

c02s05 | Evidence check | What can we really conclude?
Replication repeats a test to examine how dependable a finding is. Independent tests help us learn whether a result holds up elsewhere.

Preregistration records the planned methods and analysis before results are known. That advance record reduces some opportunities for selective reporting. Preregistration cannot rescue a poor design.

With a sound design, a larger sample can narrow the uncertainty around an estimate. More observations cannot fix a systematically unfair comparison. Sharing the study’s methods and materials lets others inspect how it was done. A memorable study is a starting point for evaluation, rather than a final verdict.

CHAPTER 3: The story that comes to mind
Learning outcome: Identify a missing base rate and formulate a question that could challenge an initial belief.

c03s01 | Story | One broken toaster becomes “they always break.”
A repaired toaster fails again. The story spreads through the café chat. Leo concludes that electrical repairs usually fail, although nobody has counted the outcomes.

Maya remembers grateful visitors and overlooks returns. Maya therefore has a more positive impression than Leo.

Both people remember genuine cases. Neither person has systematically checked all comparable repairs. To estimate how often repairs fail, the team needs a record that includes the cases nobody talked about.

c03s02 | Your decision | What would you do?
Before the explanation, make your own choice.

What most improves the estimate of repeat failures?

Consider the consequences of each option.
  1. A detailed account of the worst failure.
  2. Failures divided by comparable completed repairs.
  3. A vote on whose impression feels realistic.
  Suggested answer: 2
  Feedback: A failed repair can help explain how a fault occurs. One case cannot establish how often repairs fail.
  Feedback: The total of comparable repairs provides the denominator for a failure rate. Consistent follow-up still matters.
  Feedback: A vote measures the team’s beliefs. The voters may share the same gaps in information.

c03s03 | First lens | Memorable is not the same as common.
Heuristics are mental shortcuts. They can help when time and information are limited. The availability shortcut uses examples that come easily to mind. After hearing the toaster story, Leo may judge repair failures by how easily he remembers one.

Vivid or recent examples can be a poor guide to frequency. A complaint channel helps discover faults, but usually says little about repairs that worked.

To estimate a failure rate, count failures and the total of comparable repairs. In an invented example, four failures among forty repairs means ten percent. Forty is the denominator: the total used to interpret the four failures. Without that total and consistent follow-up, the café cannot tell how common failure is.

c03s04 | Second lens | Beliefs can filter their own evidence.
Confirmation bias means seeking or interpreting information in ways that fit an existing belief. If Leo thinks evening sessions fail, asking only about unsuccessful evenings gives his belief an unfair advantage.

Motivated reasoning concerns how a conclusion we want to reach influences our reasoning. Maya wants the café to succeed, so she may find reassuring repair stories easier to accept. The two processes can overlap; neither label proves that a conclusion is wrong.

Maya can define a failed repair in advance and follow up comparable repairs consistently. Someone with a different expectation can examine the same record. These procedures give an expectation a chance to be wrong. Calling another person biased does not replace checking the evidence.

c03s05 | Evidence check | What can we really conclude?
A bias name cannot establish why a particular person reached a particular judgment. Compare the judgment with relevant data before deciding what went wrong.

For example, a memorable set of cases might distort a judgment through availability. Or those cases might accurately reflect an expert’s experience. A representative record helps distinguish those possibilities.

Corrective strategies are useful aids. They do not make anyone immune to error. Experts also need feedback and procedures that allow expectations to be challenged.

  Suggested answer: 3

  Feedback: Agreement with Leo may reflect shared assumptions rather than independent evidence.
  Feedback: Naming outcomes that would count as success gives Leo’s belief a chance to be wrong.

CHAPTER 4: The plan we cannot let go
Learning outcome: Distinguish dissonance reduction from evidence-based revision and compare future options without counting irrecoverable costs as benefits.

c04s01 | Story | “I chose it” quietly becomes “it must be right.”
Maya championed a booking system. After weeks of work, the system still loses reservations. Maya usually spots technical problems quickly, but now explains each failure away.

For Maya, admitting that the system is poor feels like admitting that she is poor at her role. Leo proposes another month of configuration because the team has already spent so much.

Maya is defending her judgment. Leo is focused on money already spent. Both need to compare what the available options would deliver from today onward.

c04s02 | Your decision | What would you do?
Before the explanation, make your own choice.

What makes honest revision easier?

Consider the consequences of each option.
  1. Review failures and compare options afresh.
  2. Remind Maya that a competent coordinator would know better.
  3. Keep the system until the investment feels worthwhile.
  Suggested answer: 1
  Feedback: Reviewing failures and comparing options turns attention toward outcomes and the next decision.
  Feedback: Questioning Maya’s competence can increase defensiveness without improving the comparison of options.
  Feedback: Continuing with an inferior option cannot recover money that has already been spent.

c04s03 | First lens | Inconsistency can feel uncomfortable.
Cognitive dissonance theory concerns a clash between things we believe or know about our actions. “Cognitions” is the technical term for those beliefs and pieces of knowledge. The theory proposes that such a clash can create discomfort we are motivated to reduce.

Maya believes she chooses reliable tools, yet the booking system she chose keeps losing reservations. She could reduce that discomfort by dismissing each failure, or by revising her view: a capable coordinator can recognize and correct a poor choice.

People may change a behavior, change a belief, or add a justification. The response is not predetermined. How much choice and responsibility someone feels can affect the pressure to justify an action.

c04s04 | Second lens | Give the future a fresh vote.
A sunk cost is money, time, or effort already spent that cannot be recovered. The sunk cost effect occurs when that past expense influences a decision about what to do next.

The hours spent configuring the booking system cannot be recovered by continuing to use it. The next decision concerns future bookings, support costs, and the effort needed to switch. Compare the future benefits and avoidable costs of each option.

Past work can still matter through its future consequences. Skills the team can reuse are a benefit; retraining after a switch is a future cost. Those consequences belong in the comparison. The wish to make past effort feel worthwhile does not, by itself, favor continuing.

c04s05 | Evidence check | What can we really conclude?
Evidence for specific cognitive dissonance demonstrations is uneven. In 2024, researchers repeated one procedure across multiple laboratories. Participants were asked to argue for a position they did not hold, with more or less choice about doing so. This is called an induced-compliance procedure.

The study tested whether the amount of choice affected later attitudes. Its primary analysis did not support the predicted difference between the higher-choice and lower-choice groups.

That result challenges the particular procedure tested. It does not settle every version of cognitive dissonance theory. Distinguish the broad explanation of discomfort and justification from the evidence for a particular prediction. The linked Vaidis and colleagues study provides the details.

  Suggested answer: 2

  Feedback: Under the stated assumptions, switching costs one hundred pounds and keeping the tool costs three hundred. Real choices also compare risks.
  Feedback: Discomfort about switching may matter to the people involved, but it does not replace the comparison of future costs and benefits.

CHAPTER 5: The quiet vote
Learning outcome: Distinguish normative and informational influence and design a process that makes independent concerns visible.

c05s01 | Story | Everyone says yes. Has everyone agreed?
A donor offers a publicity event. The chair calls it a wonderful opportunity, and the first three volunteers agree. Sam doubts that the team can manage the workload, but stays silent.

Sam assumes Maya has checked the risks. Maya reads Sam’s silence as reassurance. Each person is taking the other’s outward response as a sign that the proposal has been considered carefully.

Public agreement can hide private doubts. Later, a request to skip an equipment check raises a different question: when should authority be challenged?

c05s02 | Your decision | What would you do?
Before the explanation, make your own choice.

What could reveal concerns before discussion?

Consider the consequences of each option.
  1. Have the chair announce the preferred answer.
  2. Collect independent written judgments first.
  3. Ask only experienced members to speak.
  Suggested answer: 2
  Feedback: Announcing the chair’s preferred answer first can pull later comments toward it, making independent concerns harder to see.
  Feedback: Independent written judgments let people record a view before seeing which answer has public support.
  Feedback: Experience matters, but other members may have relevant information.

c05s03 | First lens | Two reasons to follow a group.
Informational influence means treating other people’s responses as evidence about reality. Sam might think, “Maya agreed, so perhaps she knows we can manage the event.” This is especially relevant when Sam is uncertain.

Normative influence concerns acceptance and the wish to avoid rejection. Sam might instead think, “I’m worried about the workload, but I don’t want the team to see me as unhelpful.”

Both influences can operate at once. Sam may doubt their own estimate and fear disappointing the chair. Following a group can support useful coordination. Check whether the group’s information is sound and whether people can disagree without a social penalty.

c05s04 | Second lens | Authority changes the pressure.
Compliance means responding to a request. If Leo asks Sam to cover a shift and Sam agrees, Sam has complied. Obedience means responding to an authority’s order. Following a chair’s instruction because the chair is in charge illustrates obedience.

A request from an authority can involve both processes. The role may make a demand feel legitimate or encourage someone to think, “The chair is responsible for this decision.” That pressure does not make obedience inevitable. People can resist, and the surrounding conditions matter.

At the café, an explicit rule allows any volunteer to stop unsafe work. An agreed route for raising the concern gives that volunteer a practical next step after questioning an instruction.

c05s05 | Evidence check | What can we really conclude?
In Asch’s line-judgment studies, participants compared line lengths after hearing a group give incorrect answers. The studies examined when people would follow a majority despite the visible evidence.

In Milgram’s studies, an experimenter urged participants to continue a task they believed delivered electric shocks to another person. The studies examined responses to apparent authority in a highly structured setting.

Both sets of studies raise questions about methods, ethics, and how widely their findings apply. Their results concern particular conditions of influence; they are not universal obedience rates. Some participants resisted, and responses varied with the conditions. Harmful conduct is not inevitable.

CHAPTER 6: The invitation
Learning outcome: Evaluate a message by its evidence, audience needs, and honest description of social norms.

c06s01 | Story | Two invitations. Different reasons to care.
The café needs volunteers. Leo drafts a glossy invitation featuring a local celebrity. Maya’s invitation explains what volunteers can learn, how much time a shift takes, and what support is available.

A hurried passerby might notice the celebrity. A person deciding whether to volunteer regularly needs practical details about the commitment.

An invitation can attract attention and help someone evaluate a decision. The team needs to consider both jobs, while keeping the invitation honest.

c06s02 | Your decision | What would you do?
Before the explanation, make your own choice.

A potential volunteer worries about the time commitment. What information would help most?

Consider the consequences of each option.
  1. A second celebrity endorsement.
  2. Shift length, support, and an optional trial.
  3. A stronger claim that this is unmissable.
  Suggested answer: 2
  Feedback: A celebrity endorsement may attract attention while leaving the volunteer’s time concern unanswered.
  Feedback: Shift length, support, and an optional trial give the volunteer relevant information about the commitment.
  Feedback: Calling the opportunity unmissable adds pressure without answering how much time it requires.

c06s03 | First lens | Attention and evaluation are different jobs.
An attitude is an evaluation: for example, judging volunteering to be worthwhile or burdensome. The elaboration likelihood model describes how motivation and ability affect how carefully someone examines a persuasive message.

Central processing involves examining relevant arguments. A potential volunteer might compare the shift length, available support, and likely contribution. When someone can and wants to think carefully, argument quality matters more.

Peripheral processing gives more weight to cues, such as a familiar face or an apparently credible speaker. A hurried passerby might feel positive about the café because a favorite local celebrity supports it, without checking the practical details.

These are differences in how a message is processed, not fixed personality types or two sealed categories. The same person can process different messages more or less carefully.

c06s04 | Second lens | A norm must be true to be useful.
A descriptive norm concerns what people do. Saying that many residents volunteer communicates a descriptive norm. An injunctive norm concerns approval. Saying that the community values volunteering communicates an injunctive norm.

Both claims need evidence. Inventing a norm is misleading. Highlighting how common an unwanted behavior is can sometimes make that behavior seem normal.

For the café invitation, state what is known, explain the contribution needed, and make questions or refusal easy. Help the reader understand the commitment before choosing.

c06s05 | Evidence check | What can we really conclude?
Persuasion depends on the audience, topic, source, and outcome being measured. A changed survey answer is not the same outcome as sustained behavior change.

Treat a revised invitation as a hypothesis to test. An increase in clicks does not establish that people understood the commitment or will continue volunteering.

Compare comprehension, informed sign-ups, and retention. Ask whether people understood the effort involved and felt able to decline. Those measures are closer to the decision the café needs to support.

CHAPTER 7: When “we” takes over
Learning outcome: Identify a plausible group process behind a failure and select a procedure that addresses it.

c07s01 | Story | Two teams stop sharing a table.
Repair volunteers call the welcome desk “the talkers.” The welcome team calls the repair bench “the gatekeepers.” Each team notices its own effort more clearly than the other team’s contribution.

At a planning meeting, speakers propose increasingly ambitious events. Meanwhile, nobody owns the checklist. Each volunteer assumes someone else is responsible.

The café now faces several group problems: boundaries between teams, an escalating decision, and unclear task ownership. Each problem may need a different remedy.

c07s02 | Your decision | What would you do?
Before the explanation, make your own choice.

The shared checklist is unfinished. What is a useful first change?

Consider the consequences of each option.
  1. Agree on owners, deadlines, and needed support.
  2. Tell everyone their commitment is disappointing.
  3. Copy more people onto every task.
  Suggested answer: 1
  Feedback: Agreed owners, deadlines, and support make responsibility clearer and help people coordinate the work.
  Feedback: Telling everyone to be more committed still leaves the team unsure who owns each checklist task.
  Feedback: More recipients may strengthen the assumption that someone else will act.

c07s03 | First lens | Identity changes the boundary of “us.”
Group memberships help people understand who they are and who belongs with them. An in-group is a group someone sees as “us.” Giving repair volunteers first choice of shifts simply because they are “our team” would illustrate in-group favoritism.

Favoritism can produce unequal treatment without explicit hatred of outsiders. At the café, a shared goal—a successful visitor experience—can help the repair and welcome teams see why both contributions matter.

A shared team name alone will not resolve conflicting incentives, unequal workloads, or unequal decision power. If repair volunteers still control the rota, renaming everyone “the café team” leaves that power difference in place. Examine the arrangements that sustain the division.

c07s04 | Second lens | Groups can amplify—or lose—effort.
Group polarization means moving toward a more extreme version of a group’s initial leaning. A team initially favoring a small event might finish discussion demanding a week-long festival. Supporting arguments and comparisons with enthusiastic members can contribute. Simply agreeing on the small event would be consensus, not polarization.

Social loafing means reducing individual effort when working collectively. Someone might check fewer items because their contribution will be hidden in the team’s total.

Poor output can also reflect coordination problems. Two willing volunteers may duplicate one repair while another waits for a missing tool. That wastes genuine effort. An unfinished checklist alone does not establish loafing; check effort, tools, sequencing, and roles before choosing a remedy.

c07s05 | Evidence check | What can we really conclude?
Groupthink describes cases where pressure for agreement interferes with critical discussion. The label does not fully explain every poor group decision.

Look for an observable process. Were doubts suppressed? Was the same information repeated while other evidence was missed? Were roles or incentives unclear?

Different processes need different remedies. Independent estimates can broaden the evidence. Clear roles can improve coordination. Structured disagreement can make concerns easier to express.

  1. Ask members to show stronger enthusiasm.
  2. Gather independent estimates and contrary evidence.
  3. Open with the chair’s preferred budget.
  Suggested answer: 2
  Feedback: Rewarding public enthusiasm can encourage visible commitment without improving the demand estimate.
  Feedback: Independent estimates and contrary evidence give the group information beyond its existing enthusiasm.
  Feedback: The chair’s opening budget can become an anchor: a starting number that pulls later estimates toward it, even when demand has not been checked.

CHAPTER 8: The invisible doorway
Learning outcome: Distinguish stereotypes, prejudice, and discrimination and locate a procedural barrier to access.

c08s01 | Story | “Anyone can join.” The form says otherwise.
The café says everyone is welcome. Yet recruitment runs through existing friends, meetings happen during working hours, and the application assumes that people own specialist tools.

A newcomer is treated as inexperienced before anyone checks the applicant’s skills. The barrier may involve a judgment about the person, a recruitment rule, or both.

To understand unequal access, examine beliefs, attitudes, individual decisions, and procedures. Good intentions alone do not tell us how the process works.

c08s02 | Your decision | What would you do?
Before the explanation, make your own choice.

How should the team investigate unequal access?

Consider the consequences of each option.
  1. Ask whether organizers consider themselves fair.
  2. Trace who can enter each stage and where people drop out.
  3. Add a fairness slogan to the poster.
  Suggested answer: 2
  Feedback: Organizers’ beliefs about their own fairness do not establish who can use the recruitment process.
  Feedback: Tracing each stage can reveal access barriers that intentions and slogans miss.
  Feedback: The slogan does not change the entry requirements.

c08s03 | First lens | Belief, feeling, and action are distinct.
A stereotype is a generalized belief about a group: for example, assuming that every newcomer lacks repair skills. Prejudice is an attitude toward a group, often involving dislike: resenting newcomers before meeting them. Discrimination is unequal treatment based on group membership: rejecting an applicant because they are a newcomer, without checking relevant skills.

These examples concern beliefs, attitudes, and actions respectively. The three can overlap, but one does not establish everything about the others. A person can support equality yet use an unexamined stereotype when assessing an applicant.

A procedure can also favor an existing group through its recruitment network. Examine the actual decision, relevant comparisons, and context. Good intentions alone do not establish that a process is fair.

c08s04 | Second lens | Change the encounter and the structure.
Intergroup contact means interaction between members of different groups. It can reduce prejudice on average, though bringing people together does not guarantee improvement. Cooperation, common goals, equal status within the encounter, and institutional support are important conditions to consider.

For example, experienced volunteers and newcomers could work together on a repair, share decisions, and receive support from the coordinator. If newcomers only watch while established members control the tools, being in the same room offers less equal participation.

Contact also leaves some barriers untouched. A friendly social event does not change a rule requiring applicants to own expensive tools. Removing that requirement, where unnecessary, is a procedural change. Evaluate whether people can actually enter and participate.

c08s05 | Evidence check | What can we really conclude?
An explicit attitude is an evaluation someone reports directly. An implicit association is a link between ideas that may be measured indirectly, such as through the speed of responses in a sorting task. Discriminatory behavior concerns how people are actually treated.

Those are different things to measure. An implicit-association score is not a complete diagnosis of character or a forecast of every action. A statement of fairness also cannot establish fair treatment.

Evaluate changes using meaningful outcomes: who can apply, progress through recruitment, and participate afterward? Include follow-up and the experiences of people affected. Interpret small samples cautiously and protect privacy. Investigating unequal access does not justify collecting unnecessary personal information.

  Suggested answer: 3

  Feedback: Wording can help, but outcomes still matter.
  Feedback: Applications, progression, and participation can show where barriers remain. Interpret those outcomes with context and privacy safeguards.

CHAPTER 9: A place beside someone
Learning outcome: Explain how contact opportunities and reciprocal responsiveness can support closeness without guaranteeing it.

c09s01 | Story | Friendship begins with the quiet shift.
Sam and Leo take the quiet Thursday session. At first, they discuss only spare parts. Over several weeks, they discover a shared interest in old radios.

Leo mentions a difficult week. Sam listens before offering a solution, and Leo feels heard.

Their shared shift created opportunities to meet. Their common interest supported conversation. Sam’s responsive listening supported closeness. None of those conditions gave either person a right to the other’s friendship.

c09s02 | Your decision | What would you do?
Before the explanation, make your own choice.

A newcomer hopes to make friends. What is a respectful starting point?

Consider the consequences of each option.
  1. Offer a shared activity and notice reciprocal interest.
  2. Share increasingly private details until closeness develops.
  3. Keep arranging contact after invitations are declined.
  Suggested answer: 1
  Feedback: Offering a shared activity creates an opportunity to connect. Notice whether the other person also chooses to participate and show interest.
  Feedback: Disclosure without mutual readiness can feel intrusive.
  Feedback: A refusal is information about a boundary, not a barrier to overcome.

c09s03 | First lens | Opportunity shapes who becomes familiar.
Daily settings influence whom we have opportunities to meet. Sharing the Thursday shift makes Sam and Leo more familiar to each other. Their interest in old radios gives them something to discuss.

The mere exposure effect refers to cases where repeated encounters increase liking. A face that initially feels unfamiliar may become more comfortable to see. Repetition can also intensify annoyance or threat, so more contact is not automatically better.

Similar interests and values can make connection easier. Contact, familiarity, and similarity do not guarantee attraction, compatibility, or a healthy relationship. Sam and Leo’s shared shift creates opportunities for friendship; it does not create an obligation to become friends. Someone’s expressed boundary still matters.

c09s04 | Second lens | Closeness is a reciprocal process.
Disclosure means sharing something personal. Leo mentioning a difficult week is a disclosure. Responsiveness means responding in a way that helps someone feel understood and cared for. Sam could listen, check what Leo means, and ask whether advice would help.

Disclosure can support intimacy when it receives an understanding response. Reciprocity means that consideration can flow both ways over time; it does not require matching secrets on demand or treating favors as debts. Trust, willingness, fairness, and commitment also matter.

People differ in how secure or anxious they feel about closeness. Attachment labels describe patterns studied in relationships; they are not diagnoses. One declined invitation cannot establish a pattern or override someone’s stated wishes.

c09s05 | Evidence check | What can we really conclude?
Much relationship research examines associations or asks people to report their own experiences. Self-reports provide information about those experiences, but people may remember events differently or answer in ways they prefer others to see. Samples may also represent only particular cultures or relationship forms.

Suppose similar partners report greater satisfaction. That association does not, by itself, establish that making two people more similar would improve their relationship. Other factors might contribute to both similarity and satisfaction.

Distinguish an average association from advice for a particular pair. Ask about their experience directly. A conversation may reveal important details that a catchy compatibility rule overlooks.

CHAPTER 10: Who will do something?
Learning outcome: Identify barriers to helping and design a specific, safe response with clear responsibility.

c10s01 | Story | A crash. A full room. A moment of hesitation.
A shelf falls during a busy café session, and a visitor needs help. People hesitate while checking everyone else’s reaction.

Maya names the problem. Maya asks Leo to contact the appropriate emergency service and clears space for a trained helper.

Later, two volunteers argue about blame. The team needs to review both the immediate response and the conditions supporting cooperation. Good intentions need a clear, safe path to action.

c10s02 | Your decision | What would you do?
Before the explanation, make your own choice.

Why could concerned bystanders hesitate?

Consider the consequences of each option.
  1. Concern disappears when groups become large.
  2. People may be uncertain and assume someone else will act.
  3. Hesitation proves nobody cares.
  Suggested answer: 2
  Feedback: Group size does not determine concern on its own.
  Feedback: People may be unsure whether help is needed, or think someone else is responsible for acting.
  Feedback: A person may care and still hesitate because they do not know what happened, lack the skills to help, or fear making things worse.

c10s03 | First lens | Helping has several decision points.
The bystander effect describes conditions where the presence of others reduces an individual’s likelihood of helping. One possible process is diffusion of responsibility: “There are plenty of people here; someone else will act.” Each person may feel less personally responsible.

Pluralistic ignorance concerns interpreting others’ reactions: “Everyone looks calm, so perhaps no help is needed.” Several worried people may each hide their uncertainty and mistake everyone else’s outward calm for reassurance.

The first explanation concerns who should act; the second concerns whether action seems necessary. Both could contribute to hesitation after the shelf falls. In other emergencies, people provide essential support and increase the chance that someone helps. The effect depends on the situation.

c10s04 | Second lens | Make constructive action easier.
Aggression is behavior intended to harm. Anger is a feeling; aggression involves an intention to harm through behavior. Someone can feel angry about the fallen shelf and still respond constructively.

Frustration, perceived threat, situational pressure, and learned expectations about how conflicts are handled can contribute to aggression. Those influences do not make harm inevitable or remove responsibility.

De-escalation means reducing tension and the risk of harm. At the café, space to calm down, clear conduct rules, and a trained person to contact may help. Evaluate these arrangements rather than treating them as guarantees.

Cooperation also needs clear contributions, a way to report problems, and a fair division of benefits and burdens. Naming who checks a replacement shelf turns a general wish to cooperate into a specific responsibility.

c10s05 | Evidence check | What can we really conclude?
Laboratory studies may measure aggression through controlled tasks, such as choosing an unpleasant noise for another participant. Such measures are imperfect indicators of serious violence outside the laboratory.

Bystander findings also vary with danger, relationships, and available support. “Crowds don’t help” hides those differences and cannot explain every incident.

For a real emergency, follow local guidance and your training. A social psychology explanation does not replace first-aid instruction. Evaluate practical changes by whether appropriate, safe help becomes more available. A team saying it cares does not establish that its response plan works.
