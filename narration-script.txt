CHAPTER 1: The person and the room
Learning outcome: Given an ambiguous action, generate two situational explanations and identify evidence that distinguishes them from a personal explanation.

c01s01 | Story | Maya arrives late. The room has already decided why.
A neighborhood team is turning an empty shop into a repair café. At the first meeting, Maya arrives twenty minutes late. Leo takes her lateness as a sign that she doesn’t care about the project.

What Leo hasn’t seen is the broken lift at Maya’s apartment, or the neighbor she helped down the stairs.

Leo’s conclusion—that Maya didn’t care—was one possible explanation for her lateness. But he hadn’t considered what might have delayed her.

These characters are fictional, but the question is familiar: what did you observe, and what explanation did you supply?

c01s02 | Your decision | What would you do?
Before the explanation, make your own choice.

Leo is assigning the opening shift. What is his most informative next move?

Consider the consequences of each option.
  1. Ask Maya about today and check her availability.
  2. Use today’s lateness as the main sign of reliability.
  3. Ignore today and assume she will arrive on time.
  Suggested answer: 1
  Feedback: Asking Maya about the delay lets Leo investigate the obstacle and plan the shift without ignoring the lateness.
  Feedback: One episode may be a weak guide to a stable personal tendency.
  Feedback: Avoiding judgment is not the same as gathering evidence.

c01s03 | First lens | Behavior has more than one author.
A disposition is a relatively enduring personal tendency. A situation includes immediate demands, other people, and available choices. Social psychology examines how the person and the situation combine.

An inaccessible building may make one person late while leaving another unaffected. Considering that barrier improves our explanation of the lateness. It can also suggest a practical change, while leaving the person responsible for their commitments.

c01s04 | Second lens | Observation is not explanation.
When explaining someone else’s behavior, we can give too much weight to personal qualities and too little to circumstances. Psychologists call this tendency the fundamental attribution error. Its strength varies with culture and context.

To check your own judgment, describe the behavior neutrally. Generate alternative explanations, then look for evidence that could make each explanation less plausible. Disagreeing with someone’s judgment does not, by itself, establish that the judgment is biased.

c01s05 | Evidence check | What can we really conclude?
Attribution research describes tendencies in judgment. It does not establish that personal qualities never matter. Repeated behavior across varied settings is more informative than one episode.

Effects such as actor–observer differences depend on the judgment and the study design. Use a bias label to ask a better question about the evidence. The label alone cannot reveal what is happening in someone else’s mind.

c01s06 | New situation | Try the idea somewhere new.
Apply the idea to a new decision.

A volunteer misses shifts only when the bus service is suspended. What should you test?

Choose an action and explain why it addresses the problem.
  1. Whether personality alone predicts attendance.
  2. Whether another travel plan improves attendance.
  3. Whether the missed shifts can be deleted.
  Suggested answer: 2
  Feedback: Focusing on personality alone leaves the link between bus cancellations and missed shifts untested.
  Feedback: Trying another travel plan tests the suspected barrier while keeping the attendance expectation clear.
  Feedback: Deleting the missed shifts from the record would hide the pattern. It would not remove the travel barrier.

c01s07 | Recall | Close your notes. Explain it.
Answer both prompts from memory.

First. Explain observation versus attribution.

Second. What would strengthen a personal explanation?

Use your own example. Try both prompts before revealing the suggested answers.
  Suggested recall: An observation records an action. An attribution proposes a cause for that action.
  Suggested recall: A personal explanation becomes more credible when the pattern recurs across varied circumstances and plausible alternatives have been examined.

Here is your field practice.

Choose a mildly frustrating encounter. Write the action, your first interpretation, two alternatives, and one respectful question that could clarify it.

Return tomorrow and in a week. On each visit, answer the recall questions without notes and use a different example.

CHAPTER 2: The poster that “worked”
Learning outcome: Identify a confound in a before-and-after claim and propose a fairer comparison.

c02s01 | Story | A brighter poster. More visitors. A cause?
Leo replaces the café’s plain poster with a colorful one. Attendance rises the next weekend. Leo credits the new poster for the increase.

But other things changed too. The rain stopped, the local paper ran a story, and the opening hours changed.

The team knows that more people attended. The team does not yet know how much the poster contributed. Keep that distinction in mind whenever someone says that one change caused another.

c02s02 | Your decision | What would you do?
Before the explanation, make your own choice.

Which statement fits Leo’s evidence?

Consider the consequences of each option.
  1. The poster caused the increase.
  2. The poster had no effect.
  3. Attendance rose; several causes remain possible.
  Suggested answer: 3
  Feedback: Crediting the poster alone overlooks the weather, publicity, and opening hours that changed at the same time.
  Feedback: Uncertainty about the poster’s contribution does not establish that the poster had no effect.
  Feedback: Saying that attendance rose preserves the observation. Leaving the cause open reflects the limits of the comparison.

c02s03 | First lens | Comparison makes a claim testable.
Correlation means that two things vary together. A confound is another factor that could explain an apparent relationship. In Leo’s poster comparison, the changing weather is one possible confound.

An experiment deliberately changes a condition and compares the outcomes. For the same café event, comparable households could be randomly assigned different invitations. Random assignment helps balance other influences on average.

Random sampling answers a separate question: who is selected from the population? Even a good comparison between conditions may involve a sample that represents only a narrow group of people.

c02s04 | Second lens | Ask how much—and how uncertain.
Imagine two groups of one hundred households. Twelve households attend after receiving one invitation design. Fifteen attend after receiving the other design. These are invented teaching numbers.

The difference is three percentage points. But the estimated advantage is uncertain, and a different sample could produce a different result.

Statistical significance does not measure practical importance. A nonsignificant result does not prove zero effect. Examine the size of the estimate, its uncertainty, the design, and evidence from other tests.

c02s05 | Evidence check | What can we really conclude?
Replication repeats a test to examine how dependable a finding is. Independent tests help us learn whether a result holds up elsewhere.

Preregistration records the planned methods and analysis before results are known. That advance record reduces some opportunities for selective reporting. Preregistration cannot rescue a poor design.

Larger samples improve precision. Transparent materials let others inspect the research. A memorable study is a starting point for evaluation, rather than a final verdict.

c02s06 | New situation | Try the idea somewhere new.
Apply the idea to a new decision.

Which invitation comparison is strongest?

Choose an action and explain why it addresses the problem.
  1. Design A in winter; design B in summer.
  2. Randomize designs for one event; define attendance in advance.
  3. Design A for existing fans; design B for newcomers.
  Suggested answer: 2
  Feedback: Comparing winter with summer changes the season alongside the invitation design.
  Feedback: Randomizing invitations for one event improves the comparison. Also check whether households share invitations.
  Feedback: Existing fans and newcomers differ in prior interest. That difference could affect attendance regardless of the invitation.

c02s07 | Recall | Close your notes. Explain it.
Answer both prompts from memory.

First. How does random assignment differ from random sampling?

Second. Why is before-and-after evidence insufficient?

Use your own example. Try both prompts before revealing the suggested answers.
  Suggested recall: Random assignment allocates people to conditions. Random sampling selects people from a population.
  Suggested recall: Other conditions can change over time and produce the same before-and-after pattern.

CHAPTER 3: The story that comes to mind
Learning outcome: Identify a missing base rate and formulate a question that could challenge an initial belief.

c03s01 | Story | One broken toaster becomes “they always break.”
A repaired toaster fails again. The story spreads through the café chat. Leo concludes that electrical repairs usually fail, although nobody has counted the outcomes.

Maya remembers grateful visitors and overlooks returns. Maya therefore has a more positive impression than Leo.

Both people remember genuine cases. Neither person has systematically checked all comparable repairs. To estimate how often repairs fail, the team needs a record that includes the cases nobody talked about.

c03s02 | Your decision | What would you do?
Before the explanation, make your own choice.

What most improves the estimate of repeat failures?

Consider the consequences of each option.
  1. A detailed account of the worst failure.
  2. Failures divided by comparable completed repairs.
  3. A vote on whose impression feels realistic.
  Suggested answer: 2
  Feedback: A failed repair can help explain how a fault occurs. One case cannot establish how often repairs fail.
  Feedback: The total of comparable repairs provides the denominator for a failure rate. Consistent follow-up still matters.
  Feedback: A vote measures the team’s beliefs. The voters may share the same gaps in information.

c03s03 | First lens | Memorable is not the same as common.
Heuristics are mental shortcuts. They can help when time and information are limited. The availability shortcut uses examples that come easily to mind.

Availability can mislead when vivid or recent examples are a poor guide to frequency. A complaint channel may help you discover faults while giving a distorted picture of how common those faults are.

Ask which cases reached your attention, and which cases you never heard about. Then look for a relevant total to compare them with.

c03s04 | Second lens | Beliefs can filter their own evidence.
Confirmation bias includes seeking or interpreting information in ways that fit an existing belief. Motivated reasoning adds the influence of a conclusion we want to reach.

Start by checking your own evidence-seeking habits. Maya can define a failed repair in advance and follow up comparable repairs consistently. Maya can also ask someone with a different expectation to examine the same record.

Those procedures give an expectation a chance to be wrong. Calling another person biased is not a substitute for checking the evidence.

c03s05 | Evidence check | What can we really conclude?
A bias name cannot establish why a particular person reached a particular judgment. Compare the judgment with relevant data before deciding what went wrong.

For example, a memorable set of cases might distort a judgment through availability. Or those cases might accurately reflect an expert’s experience. A representative record helps distinguish those possibilities.

Corrective strategies are useful aids. They do not make anyone immune to error. Experts also need feedback and procedures that allow expectations to be challenged.

c03s06 | New situation | Try the idea somewhere new.
Apply the idea to a new decision.

Leo thinks evening sessions fail. Which question best challenges his belief?

Choose an action and explain why it addresses the problem.
  1. Which evenings went badly?
  2. Who agrees that evenings are a poor idea?
  3. What outcomes would make us conclude evenings work?
  Suggested answer: 3
  Feedback: Looking only at unsuccessful evening sessions selects evidence that fits Leo’s initial belief.
  Feedback: Agreement with Leo may reflect shared assumptions rather than independent evidence.
  Feedback: Naming outcomes that would count as success gives Leo’s belief a chance to be wrong.

c03s07 | Recall | Close your notes. Explain it.
Answer both prompts from memory.

First. Why does a vivid case need a denominator?

Second. When can an availability shortcut be useful?

Use your own example. Try both prompts before revealing the suggested answers.
  Suggested recall: A vivid case needs a denominator because the total of comparable cases is needed to estimate frequency.
  Suggested recall: An availability shortcut can help when the examples that come to mind reliably represent the relevant events.

Here is your field practice.

CHAPTER 4: The plan we cannot let go
Learning outcome: Distinguish dissonance reduction from evidence-based revision and compare future options without counting irrecoverable costs as benefits.

c04s01 | Story | “I chose it” quietly becomes “it must be right.”
Maya championed a booking system. After weeks of work, the system still loses reservations. Maya usually spots technical problems quickly, but now explains each failure away.

For Maya, admitting that the system is poor feels like admitting that she is poor at her role. Leo proposes another month of configuration because the team has already spent so much.

Maya is defending her judgment. Leo is focused on money already spent. Both need to compare what the available options would deliver from today onward.

c04s02 | Your decision | What would you do?
Before the explanation, make your own choice.

What makes honest revision easier?

Consider the consequences of each option.
  1. Review failures and compare options afresh.
  2. Remind Maya that a competent coordinator would know better.
  3. Keep the system until the investment feels worthwhile.
  Suggested answer: 1
  Feedback: Reviewing failures and comparing options turns attention toward outcomes and the next decision.
  Feedback: Questioning Maya’s competence can increase defensiveness without improving the comparison of options.
  Feedback: Continuing with an inferior option cannot recover money that has already been spent.

c04s03 | First lens | Inconsistency can feel uncomfortable.
Cognitive dissonance theory concerns inconsistency between cognitions, such as beliefs or knowledge about our actions. The theory proposes that this inconsistency can create discomfort that we are motivated to reduce.

A person may change a behavior, change a belief, or add a justification. The response is not predetermined. Maya could defend the booking tool, or decide that being careful includes noticing and correcting a mistake.

How much choice and responsibility someone feels can affect the pressure to justify an action.

c04s04 | Second lens | Give the future a fresh vote.
The sunk cost effect concerns decisions influenced by costs that cannot be recovered. It is related to self-justification, but it is a distinct decision problem.

Money or effort already spent should not, by itself, determine the best next option. Compare the future benefits and avoidable costs of continuing or switching.

Past investments can still matter when they change future consequences. Usable skills, remaining obligations, and switching costs belong in that comparison. Ask what you would choose if you took over the project today.

c04s05 | Evidence check | What can we really conclude?
Evidence for specific dissonance demonstrations is uneven. In 2024, a research team repeated one induced-compliance procedure across multiple laboratories. The study compared attitude change under high-choice and low-choice conditions.

The primary analysis did not support the predicted difference between those conditions. That result challenges the particular procedure tested. It does not settle every version of cognitive dissonance theory.

Keep the general theoretical framework separate from confidence in any single demonstration. The linked Vaidis and colleagues study provides the details.

c04s06 | New situation | Try the idea somewhere new.
Apply the idea to a new decision.

Switching costs £100; keeping the tool costs £300 in future support. Benefits are equal. What matters?

Choose an action and explain why it addresses the problem.
  1. The large fee already paid.
  2. The lower avoidable future cost of switching.
  3. Only the emotional discomfort of switching.
  Suggested answer: 2
  Feedback: The original fee cannot be recovered under the stated assumptions. It does not favor either future option.
  Feedback: Under the stated assumptions, switching costs one hundred pounds and keeping the tool costs three hundred. Real choices also compare risks.
  Feedback: Discomfort about switching may matter to the people involved, but it does not replace the comparison of future costs and benefits.

c04s07 | Recall | Close your notes. Explain it.
Answer both prompts from memory.

First. Name two ways to reduce dissonance.

Second. When can a past investment still matter?

Use your own example. Try both prompts before revealing the suggested answers.
  Suggested recall: People may reduce dissonance by changing a behavior or belief, or by adding a justification. Context affects the response.
  Suggested recall: A past investment matters when it changes future consequences, such as switching costs or usable skills.

c04s08 | Field practice | Carry one idea into your week.
Here is your field practice.

Revisit a small commitment you can change. List the future benefits and avoidable costs of continuing or stopping. Write a sentence that permits correction without attacking your character.

Return tomorrow and in a week. On each visit, answer the recall questions without notes and use a different example.

CHAPTER 5: The quiet vote
Learning outcome: Distinguish normative and informational influence and design a process that makes independent concerns visible.

c05s01 | Story | Everyone says yes. Has everyone agreed?
A donor offers a publicity event. The chair calls it a wonderful opportunity, and the first three volunteers agree. Sam doubts that the team can manage the workload, but stays silent.

Sam assumes Maya has checked the risks. Maya reads Sam’s silence as reassurance. Each person is taking the other’s outward response as a sign that the proposal has been considered carefully.

Public agreement can hide private doubts. Later, a request to skip an equipment check raises a different question: when should authority be challenged?

c05s02 | Your decision | What would you do?
Before the explanation, make your own choice.

What could reveal concerns before discussion?

Consider the consequences of each option.
  1. Have the chair announce the preferred answer.
  2. Collect independent written judgments first.
  3. Ask only experienced members to speak.
  Suggested answer: 2
  Feedback: Announcing the chair’s preferred answer first may anchor the comments that follow.
  Feedback: Independent written judgments let people record a view before seeing which answer has public support.
  Feedback: Experience matters, but other members may have relevant information.

c05s03 | First lens | Two reasons to follow a group.
Informational influence means treating other people’s responses as evidence about reality. It is especially relevant when we are uncertain. Normative influence concerns acceptance and the wish to avoid rejection.

Both influences can operate at once. Sam may doubt their own workload estimate and also fear disappointing the chair.

Following a group can support useful coordination. Check whether the group’s information is sound, and whether people can disagree without a social penalty.

c05s04 | Second lens | Authority changes the pressure.
Compliance means responding to a request. Obedience means responding to an authority’s order. An authority’s role can make a demand feel legitimate and shift perceived responsibility.

That pressure does not make obedience inevitable. People can resist, and the conditions surrounding an order matter.

At the café, an explicit rule allows any volunteer to stop unsafe work. An agreed escalation route gives the volunteer a practical next step after questioning the instruction.

c05s05 | Evidence check | What can we really conclude?
Asch’s line-judgment studies examined majority influence in an artificial task. Milgram’s studies examined responses to apparent authority in a highly structured setting.

To interpret those experiments, consider their methods, ethics, and limits. The findings concern particular conditions of influence. They are not universal population obedience rates.

Some participants resisted, and responses varied with the conditions. Famous demonstrations do not establish that harmful conduct is inevitable.

c05s06 | New situation | Try the idea somewhere new.
Apply the idea to a new decision.

The chair requests bypassing a required check. Which process helps?

Choose an action and explain why it addresses the problem.
  1. Let the first confident person decide.
  2. Allow anonymous complaints afterward.
  3. Pause and use an agreed safety escalation route.
  Suggested answer: 3
  Feedback: Confidence is not evidence of safety.
  Feedback: Later complaints cannot prevent unchecked work now.
  Feedback: An agreed stop-and-escalation route makes questioning possible while the safety decision still matters.

c05s07 | Recall | Close your notes. Explain it.
Answer both prompts from memory.

First. Distinguish normative and informational influence.

Second. Why can public agreement overstate private agreement?

Use your own example. Try both prompts before revealing the suggested answers.
  Suggested recall: Normative influence concerns acceptance. Informational influence treats other people’s responses as evidence.
  Suggested recall: Public agreement can hide private doubt when people copy early answers, suppress concerns, or assume that others have checked.

c05s08 | Field practice | Carry one idea into your week.
Here is your field practice.

For an agreed, low-stakes group choice, have everyone write a view before discussion. Compare the range of initial reasons with what a show of hands might have revealed.

Return tomorrow and in a week. On each visit, answer the recall questions without notes and use a different example.

CHAPTER 6: The invitation
Learning outcome: Evaluate a message by its evidence, audience needs, and honest description of social norms.

c06s01 | Story | Two invitations. Different reasons to care.
The café needs volunteers. Leo drafts a glossy invitation featuring a local celebrity. Maya’s invitation explains what volunteers can learn, how much time a shift takes, and what support is available.

A hurried passerby might notice the celebrity. A person deciding whether to volunteer regularly needs practical details about the commitment.

An invitation can attract attention and help someone evaluate a decision. The team needs to consider both jobs, while keeping the invitation honest.

c06s02 | Your decision | What would you do?
Before the explanation, make your own choice.

A potential volunteer worries about the time commitment. What information would help most?

Consider the consequences of each option.
  1. A second celebrity endorsement.
  2. Shift length, support, and an optional trial.
  3. A stronger claim that this is unmissable.
  Suggested answer: 2
  Feedback: A celebrity endorsement may attract attention while leaving the volunteer’s time concern unanswered.
  Feedback: Shift length, support, and an optional trial give the volunteer relevant information about the commitment.
  Feedback: Calling the opportunity unmissable adds pressure without answering how much time it requires.

c06s03 | First lens | Attention and evaluation are different jobs.
An attitude is an evaluation of something. The elaboration likelihood model explains how motivation and ability can affect the way a persuasive message is processed.

In central processing, a person examines relevant arguments. When someone has the motivation and attention to think carefully, argument quality matters more.

In peripheral processing, cues such as familiarity or perceived credibility can carry more weight. These routes are not fixed personality types. The same person may examine a major commitment carefully and use a shortcut for a minor decision.

c06s04 | Second lens | A norm must be true to be useful.
A descriptive norm concerns what people do. Saying that many residents volunteer communicates a descriptive norm. An injunctive norm concerns approval. Saying that the community values volunteering communicates an injunctive norm.

Both claims need evidence. Inventing a norm is misleading. Highlighting how common an unwanted behavior is can sometimes make that behavior seem normal.

For the café invitation, state what is known, explain the contribution needed, and make questions or refusal easy. Help the reader understand the commitment before choosing.

c06s05 | Evidence check | What can we really conclude?
Persuasion depends on the audience, topic, source, and outcome being measured. A changed survey answer is not the same outcome as sustained behavior change.

Treat a revised invitation as a hypothesis to test. An increase in clicks does not establish that people understood the commitment or will continue volunteering.

Compare comprehension, informed sign-ups, and retention. Ask whether people understood the effort involved and felt able to decline. Those measures are closer to the decision the café needs to support.

c06s06 | New situation | Try the idea somewhere new.
Apply the idea to a new decision.

Few residents currently volunteer. Which invitation is defensible?

Choose an action and explain why it addresses the problem.
  1. “Everyone here already volunteers.”
  2. “Hardly anyone helps—don’t be like them.”
  3. “Try a supported two-hour shift; here is what it involves.”
  Suggested answer: 3
  Feedback: Claiming that everyone volunteers invents a descriptive norm that the team cannot substantiate.
  Feedback: Highlighting how few people help can make nonparticipation seem normal. The wording also adds shame.
  Feedback: A supported two-hour trial is a concrete, checkable request. Its effect on informed participation still needs evaluation.

c06s07 | Recall | Close your notes. Explain it.
Answer both prompts from memory.

First. What makes central processing more likely?

Second. Distinguish descriptive and injunctive norms.

Use your own example. Try both prompts before revealing the suggested answers.
  Suggested recall: Central processing is more likely when someone is motivated and has the ability, attention, and information to examine the arguments.
  Suggested recall: Descriptive norms concern what people do. Injunctive norms concern approval.

Here is your field practice.

CHAPTER 7: When “we” takes over
Learning outcome: Identify a plausible group process behind a failure and select a procedure that addresses it.

c07s01 | Story | Two teams stop sharing a table.
Repair volunteers call the welcome desk “the talkers.” The welcome team calls the repair bench “the gatekeepers.” Each team notices its own effort more clearly than the other team’s contribution.

At a planning meeting, speakers propose increasingly ambitious events. Meanwhile, nobody owns the checklist. Each volunteer assumes someone else is responsible.

The café now faces several group problems: boundaries between teams, an escalating decision, and unclear task ownership. Each problem may need a different remedy.

c07s02 | Your decision | What would you do?
Before the explanation, make your own choice.

The shared checklist is unfinished. What is a useful first change?

Consider the consequences of each option.
  1. Agree on owners, deadlines, and needed support.
  2. Tell everyone their commitment is disappointing.
  3. Copy more people onto every task.
  Suggested answer: 1
  Feedback: Agreed owners, deadlines, and support make responsibility clearer and help people coordinate the work.
  Feedback: General criticism leaves allocation unclear.
  Feedback: More recipients may strengthen the assumption that someone else will act.

c07s03 | First lens | Identity changes the boundary of “us.”
Group memberships help people understand themselves and others. Favoring an in-group means giving preference to people seen as part of “our” group. That favoritism can produce unequal treatment without explicit hatred of outsiders.

At the café, the repair and welcome teams can work toward a shared goal: a successful visitor experience. That goal makes each team’s contribution easier to see.

A shared team name alone will not resolve conflicting incentives, unequal workloads, or unequal decision power. Look at the structure that keeps the division in place.

c07s04 | Second lens | Groups can amplify—or lose—effort.
Group polarization is movement toward a more extreme position from an initial leaning. Discussion can supply supporting arguments and encourage comparisons with other members. Polarization is different from simply reaching agreement.

Social loafing means reduced individual effort in a collective task. It can become more likely when individual contributions are hard to identify.

Poor group output can also reflect coordination problems. Missing tools, duplicated work, or unclear roles can waste genuine effort. An unfinished checklist does not, by itself, establish that people are loafing.

c07s05 | Evidence check | What can we really conclude?
Groupthink describes cases where pressure for agreement interferes with critical discussion. The label does not fully explain every poor group decision.

Look for an observable process. Were doubts suppressed? Was the same information repeated while other evidence was missed? Were roles or incentives unclear?

Different processes need different remedies. Independent estimates can broaden the evidence. Clear roles can improve coordination. Structured disagreement can make concerns easier to express.

c07s06 | New situation | Try the idea somewhere new.
Apply the idea to a new decision.

An event budget keeps escalating without checking demand. What helps?

Choose an action and explain why it addresses the problem.
  1. Ask members to show stronger enthusiasm.
  2. Gather independent estimates and contrary evidence.
  3. Open with the chair’s preferred budget.
  Suggested answer: 2
  Feedback: Rewarding public enthusiasm can encourage visible commitment without improving the demand estimate.
  Feedback: Independent estimates and contrary evidence give the group information beyond its existing enthusiasm.
  Feedback: A preferred opening number can anchor later judgments.

c07s07 | Recall | Close your notes. Explain it.
Answer both prompts from memory.

First. How does polarization differ from consensus?

Second. How can a willing group still perform poorly?

Use your own example. Try both prompts before revealing the suggested answers.
  Suggested recall: Consensus is agreement. Polarization is movement toward a more extreme position from an initial leaning.
  Suggested recall: A willing group can still lose output through poor sequencing, duplicated work, missing tools, or unclear roles.

c07s08 | Field practice | Carry one idea into your week.
Here is your field practice.

Return tomorrow and in a week. On each visit, answer the recall questions without notes and use a different example.

CHAPTER 8: The invisible doorway
Learning outcome: Distinguish stereotypes, prejudice, and discrimination and locate a procedural barrier to access.

c08s01 | Story | “Anyone can join.” The form says otherwise.
The café says everyone is welcome. Yet recruitment runs through existing friends, meetings happen during working hours, and the application assumes that people own specialist tools.

A newcomer is treated as inexperienced before anyone checks the applicant’s skills. The barrier may involve a judgment about the person, a recruitment rule, or both.

To understand unequal access, examine beliefs, attitudes, individual decisions, and procedures. Good intentions alone do not tell us how the process works.

c08s02 | Your decision | What would you do?
Before the explanation, make your own choice.

How should the team investigate unequal access?

Consider the consequences of each option.
  1. Ask whether organizers consider themselves fair.
  2. Trace who can enter each stage and where people drop out.
  3. Add a fairness slogan to the poster.
  Suggested answer: 2
  Feedback: Organizers’ beliefs about their own fairness do not establish who can use the recruitment process.
  Feedback: Tracing each stage can reveal access barriers that intentions and slogans miss.
  Feedback: The slogan does not change the entry requirements.

c08s03 | First lens | Belief, feeling, and action are distinct.
A stereotype is a generalized belief about a group. Prejudice is an evaluative attitude toward a group. Discrimination is unequal treatment based on group membership.

The three concepts overlap, but they are distinct. A person can endorse equality and still use an unexamined stereotype when assessing an applicant. A procedure can also favor an existing group through its recruitment network.

Examine the actual decision, relevant comparisons, and context. An ambiguous action cannot reveal someone’s entire belief system, and positive intentions cannot establish that a process is fair.

c08s04 | Second lens | Change the encounter and the structure.
Intergroup contact can reduce prejudice on average. Important conditions to consider include cooperation, common goals, equal status within the encounter, and institutional support.

Simply bringing people together is not a guarantee of improvement. At the café, meaningful participation includes access to resources and a real voice in decisions.

A social event may improve an encounter while leaving entry requirements unchanged. Procedural barriers need procedural changes. Evaluate whether people can actually enter and participate.

c08s05 | Evidence check | What can we really conclude?
Explicit attitudes, implicit associations, and discriminatory behavior are different things to measure. A score on an implicit-association measure is not a complete diagnosis of a person’s character or a forecast of every action.

Evaluate an intervention using meaningful behavior and access outcomes. Include follow-up and the experiences of people affected by the process.

Interpret small samples cautiously and protect privacy. Investigating unequal access does not justify collecting unnecessary personal information.

CHAPTER 9: A place beside someone
Learning outcome: Explain how contact opportunities and reciprocal responsiveness can support closeness without guaranteeing it.

c09s01 | Story | Friendship begins with the quiet shift.
Sam and Leo take the quiet Thursday session. At first, they discuss only spare parts. Over several weeks, they discover a shared interest in old radios.

Leo mentions a difficult week. Sam listens before offering a solution, and Leo feels heard.

Their shared shift created opportunities to meet. Their common interest supported conversation. Sam’s responsive listening supported closeness. None of those conditions gave either person a right to the other’s friendship.

Before the explanation, make your own choice.

Consider the consequences of each option.
  1. Offer a shared activity and notice reciprocal interest.
  2. Share increasingly private details until closeness develops.
  3. Keep arranging contact after invitations are declined.
  Suggested answer: 1
  Feedback: Offering a shared activity creates an opportunity to connect while preserving the other person’s choice.
  Feedback: Disclosure without mutual readiness can feel intrusive.
  Feedback: A refusal is information about a boundary, not a barrier to overcome.

c09s03 | First lens | Opportunity shapes who becomes familiar.
Daily settings influence who you have opportunities to meet. Shared schedules can make particular people familiar.

Mere exposure describes cases where repeated encounters increase liking. Repetition can also intensify annoyance or threat, so more contact is not automatically better. Similar interests and values can make connection easier.

Contact, familiarity, and similarity do not guarantee attraction, compatibility, or a healthy relationship. Someone’s expressed boundary still matters.

c09s04 | Second lens | Closeness is a reciprocal process.
Disclosure means sharing something personal. Disclosure can support intimacy when the other person responds with understanding and appropriate care.

Reciprocity does not require matching secrets on demand or treating favors as debts. Trust, willingness, support, fairness, and commitment also shape a relationship.

People differ in how secure or anxious they feel about closeness. Attachment labels are not diagnoses. A label should not be used to explain away what a person says they want.

c09s05 | Evidence check | What can we really conclude?
Much relationship research is correlational or uses self-reports. Samples may represent particular cultures and relationship forms.

Suppose similar partners report greater satisfaction. That association does not, by itself, establish that making two people more similar would improve their relationship.

Distinguish an average association from advice for a particular pair. Direct communication provides information that a catchy compatibility rule may miss.

CHAPTER 10: Who will do something?
Learning outcome: Identify barriers to helping and design a specific, safe response with clear responsibility.

c10s01 | Story | A crash. A full room. A moment of hesitation.
A shelf falls during a busy café session, and a visitor needs help. People hesitate while checking everyone else’s reaction.

Maya names the problem. Maya asks Leo to contact the appropriate emergency service and clears space for a trained helper.

Later, two volunteers argue about blame. The team needs to review both the immediate response and the conditions supporting cooperation. Good intentions need a clear, safe path to action.

c10s02 | Your decision | What would you do?
Before the explanation, make your own choice.

Why could concerned bystanders hesitate?

Consider the consequences of each option.
  1. Concern disappears when groups become large.
  2. People may be uncertain and assume someone else will act.
  3. Hesitation proves nobody cares.
  Suggested answer: 2
  Feedback: Group size does not determine concern on its own.
  Feedback: Ambiguity and divided responsibility can both contribute.
  Feedback: Ability, safety, and uncertainty are alternative explanations.

c10s03 | First lens | Helping has several decision points.
The bystander effect describes conditions where the presence of others reduces an individual’s likelihood of helping. Diffusion of responsibility means that each person feels less personally responsible for acting.

Pluralistic ignorance concerns interpretation. People may read others’ outward calm as evidence that nothing is wrong, even when others are also uncertain.

Those patterns depend on the situation. In some emergencies, other people provide essential support and increase the chance that someone helps. Crowds do not mechanically remove concern.

c10s04 | Second lens | Make constructive action easier.
Aggression is behavior intended to harm. Frustration, learned scripts, perceived threat, and situational pressure can contribute to aggression. Anger does not inevitably lead to harm.

At the café, calm space, clear conduct rules, and a trained escalation route can support a constructive response. These are practical arrangements to evaluate, rather than guarantees.

Cooperation also needs clear expectations about contributions, reporting problems, and sharing benefits and burdens. Context can improve an explanation while leaving accountability for harmful actions intact.

c10s05 | Evidence check | What can we really conclude?
Laboratory aggression measures are imperfect indicators of serious violence. Bystander findings also vary with danger, relationships, and available support. A slogan about crowds can hide those differences.

For a real emergency, follow local guidance and your training. A social psychology explanation does not replace first-aid instruction.

Evaluate a practical change by whether appropriate, safe help becomes more available. Concern alone does not establish that a response plan works.
